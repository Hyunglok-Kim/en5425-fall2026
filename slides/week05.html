Part 1 · Gradients through time

Recurrence shares weights across time — and multiplies its gradient

Why a 150-step memory needs a gradient that survives 150 steps.  ·  Where every recurrent model — and it is why Transformers exist (Week 6).  ·  Do compute the factor once; never trust a plain RNN with long memory.

  • A recurrent neural network (RNN) reads one step at a time and carries a state: ht = tanh(W·ht−1 + U·xt + b). The same W and U at every step — last week a convolution shared weights across space; this shares them across time.
  • Training unrolls the loop and backpropagates through it. The gradient that reaches step t is a product with one factor per step in between: ∂hT/∂ht = ∏ diag(1 − hk²)·W.
  • A factor of 0.9 per day over 150 days: 0.9¹⁵⁰ ≈ 1.4 × 10⁻⁷. A factor of 1.1: 1.6 × 10⁶. It vanishes or it explodes; it almost never survives.
  • Exploding has a fix you already own — clip the gradient norm (Week 2). Vanishing has no clipping fix — the path has to change, or be gated and initialized with care.
ht — the hidden state: a fixed-size summary of everything read so far. It is the only thing that crosses from one step to the next. xt — the input at step t: here one day of rain, temperature and radiation for the basin. BPTT “backpropagation through time” — ordinary backpropagation on the unrolled chain; nothing new, except that the chain is as long as the sequence. diag(1 − h²)·W — the derivative of one step: tanh's slope times the recurrent weight. Its size, roughly its largest singular value, decides vanish or explode. in the figure — forward, left to right, the same W every time; backward, right to left, the orange gradient thins by a factor at each step.
2.5 min. Two moves. First the weight-sharing callback: Week 4 said a convolution is the same filter at every position; an RNN is the same cell at every time step — same argument, other axis. Second the arithmetic, done out loud: 0.9 to the 150th is about 10⁻⁷, 1.1 to the 150th is about 10⁶ — ask the room which one is worse, then say both, because one silently stops learning and the other crashes. The honest precision: it is the spectral norm of the step Jacobian that matters, not a scalar, and tanh's slope is at most 1, so the typical case is vanishing — full derivation in Appendix A1 (Pascanu et al. 2013). Transition: so we need a path along which the gradient is not multiplied by W at every step.

Part 1 · The LSTM

The LSTM adds to its memory instead of rewriting it

Why it replaces the product with a gate the network controls.  ·  Where Kratzert’s CAMELS models, Google Flood Hub, today’s lab; last week’s ConvLSTM.  ·  Do name the lane and the three gates on any LSTM diagram.

ct = ft ⊙ ct−1 + it ⊙ gt   ·   ht = ot ⊙ tanh(ct)

  • Along the lane the direct step derivative is just ∂ct/∂ct−1 = ft (gates held fixed) — a gate the network sets, not W squashed through tanh. When f ≈ 1, December’s error signal reaches April intact.
  • The gated recurrent unit (GRU) merges the lane into the hidden state with two gates; on small data it usually ties. Last week’s ConvLSTM is this cell with every matrix product turned into a convolution.
LSTM “long short-term memory” — Hochreiter & Schmidhuber 1997; the forget gate was added by Gers, Schmidhuber & Cummins 2000. f, i, o — forget, input and output gates, each σ(W·[ht−1, xt] + b): a number between 0 and 1 per memory cell. g — the candidate content, a tanh of the same inputs. ⊙ — elementwise product: each memory cell is gated on its own. in the figure — the orange lane is the cell state; everything that touches it is a gate multiplication or an addition, which is why gradients survive along it.
2.5 min. Draw only the orange lane on the board, then the two operations on it: times f, plus i·g. The one line to land: backward along the lane the factor is f, which the network learns to hold near 1 for as long as it needs the memory — Hochreiter and Schmidhuber called it the constant error carrousel (their spelling). Contrast with the previous slide: there the factor was W times tanh's slope at every step, not under anyone's control. GRU in one sentence, no equations (Appendix A2). The hydrologist's reading, which sets up the next slide: the lane is a storage — snowpack, soil water — the input gate is accumulation, the forget gate is loss, the output gate is release. That is a bucket model, learned.

Part 1 · The CAMELS story

How much of tomorrow’s river is already in today’s?

  • CAMELS (Catchment Attributes and MEteorology for Large-sample Studies) — 671 US basins, 35 years of daily basin-mean weather, USGS streamflow and catchment attributes: hydrology’s ImageNet, and your lab.

Part 1 · The two metrics

NSE and KGE — and what doing nothing scores on each

Why the same do-nothing forecast scores 0.48 on one metric and 0.74 on the other.  ·  Where every hydrology paper; Note 05; the capstone hydrology track.  ·  Do put the benchmark row on the metric you report.

NSE = 1 − Σ(q̂t − qt)² / Σ(qt − q̄)²  1 is perfect · 0 is “as good as the observed mean” · below 0 is worse than the mean

NSE “Nash–Sutcliffe efficiency” (1970) — the field’s default score. KGE “Kling–Gupta efficiency” (Gupta et al. 2009) — splits the error into timing, spread and bias. why persistence wins on KGE — a copy of yesterday has exactly the right mean and spread (α = β = 1), so KGE only sees its lag-1 correlation. −0.41 — any constant forecast scores KGE ≤ 1 − √2 ≈ −0.41 (taking r = 0, since a constant has no correlation), so a KGE of 0 is not “no skill”, unlike an NSE of 0 (Knoben et al. 2019).

Part 2 · Two regimes

Why the model you train and the forecast you run are different objects.  ·  Where GraphCast (40 self-fed steps), the W11 lab (12), today’s lab (15).  ·  Do score skill at every lead time, never at one.

  • Autoregressive: ŷt+1 = f(yt, xt+1); a k-step forecast feeds f its own output k times. GraphCast steps 6 hours at a time, so its 10-day forecast is 40 of its own outputs fed back in.

Part 2 · The error recursion, derived

Why errors pile up: one line, unrolled

Why it separates the two things that can be wrong — the step (σ²) or the feedback (a) — and the one-step score shows only one.  ·  Where the error-growth step inside every data-assimilation cycle (Week 3); predictability limits; every rollout diagnostic in Week 11.  ·  Do know your a.

Truth: yk+1 = f(yk) − δk+1  δ = the model’s one-step error
Forecast: ŷk+1 = f(ŷk). Subtract, linearize with a = ∂f/∂y:
ek+1 ≈ a·ek + δk+1  — unroll it: e2 = aδ1 + δ2,  e3 = a²δ1 + aδ2 + δ3
so ek = Σj ak−j δj  ⇒  Var(ek) = σ² (1 − a2k) / (1 − a²)
  • |a| < 1 — saturates at σ²/(1 − a²): the system forgets old errors, as a river does. a = 1 — grows like k. |a| > 1 — grows like a2k: an unstable model, or a chaotic atmosphere.
  • The one-step score reports σ². It hides how a compounds it. Our river model has a = 0.911, so its error variance settles near 5.9× the one-step variance.
ek — the forecast error k steps into the run, ŷk − yk. δ — the fresh error one step adds even from a perfect input, exactly what teacher forcing measures; the variance line assumes the δ’s are independent with variance σ². a — how much of an input error survives one step: the model's slope, ∂f/∂y. For a vector state it is the Jacobian matrix and its largest eigenvalues play the role of |a|. 5.9× — 1/(1 − 0.911²) for our river model: the long-lead error variance, relative to the one-step variance that the training loss saw. in the figure — identical one-step error in all three curves. What separates saturate, crawl and explode is a, which the one-step loss never looked at.
3.5 min, and this is the board derivation of the deck — write it, do not read it. Three lines: truth equals model step minus one-step error; subtract the forecast; linearize — e_{k+1} = a·e_k + δ. Then unroll twice by hand (e₂ = aδ₁ + δ₂, e₃ = a²δ₁ + aδ₂ + δ₃) until the geometric sum is obvious, and write the variance. The payoff sentence, underlined: the one-step score reports σ² and hides a — for our linear model a is even a fitted coefficient, and the score still says nothing about what it does over 15 steps. That is discussion question 2 in one line, so stop there — the remedies are theirs to argue. The honest caveats if asked: δ's are not independent in practice (errors are correlated in time) and a varies along the trajectory; the three regimes survive both. Chaos is |a| > 1 in the true system itself, which is why weather has a predictability limit no model removes. Full vector version: Appendix A6.

  • The one-step loss pins f down on the data. The slope a that the recursion multiplies is set by what f does around the data, where the rollout wanders — and nothing in training looked there.

  • Named in machine translation (Ranzato et al. 2016). Weather models show it as drift of the global mean, blow-up after a week or two, or noise that grows at the smallest scales.

Part 2 · Remedy: train on the rollout

Put the rollout inside the loss

Why the only fix that lets the loss see a.  ·  Where FourCastNet’s 2-step fine-tune, GraphCast’s 12-step curriculum, your lab’s K = 3.  ·  Do implement it — five lines.

y_hat = y[t]                  # start from the truth
loss = 0
for k in range(1, K + 1):     # K = 3 in the lab
    y_hat = model(y_hat, x[t + k])   # its own output: the line the lab changes
    loss = loss + mse(y_hat, y[t + k])   # state and graph stay attached
(loss / K).backward()         # gradients through all K steps
  • The loss now sees a: an error made at step 1 is paid for again at steps 2 … K, so the gradient pushes the model toward states it can recover from.
  • The bill: memory and compute grow with K — every intermediate state is kept for the backward pass, Week 2’s activation-memory debt paid K times.
  • On our linear river model a 3-step loss moved a from 0.911 to 0.901 and the 15-day NSE from 0.35 to 0.37 — small, because a stable linear model has almost nothing to fix. The lab asks whether an LSTM’s gap is bigger.
K — the rollout length inside the loss. K = 1 is teacher forcing; the lab compares K = 1 with K = 3. backpropagating through the rollout — the gradient of step 3's error flows back through step 2's and step 1's model calls: BPTT again, over forecast steps instead of input days. why start small — from random weights a long rollout is noise feeding noise, and its gradient is useless. Learn the step first, then the rollout; the weather models on the next slide make this a schedule.
Rollout losses in the weather literature: FourCastNet (two-step fine-tuning), GraphCast (up to 12 steps) · cheaper relatives — input noise, scheduled sampling, the pushforward trick: Appendix A7
1.7 min. Read the code's three comments and nothing else: start from the truth, feed your own output, backpropagate through all of it. The honest number is the point of the third bullet — for our linear model the fix barely moves the curve, and that is a prediction, not a disappointment: exposure bias needs a model flexible enough to exploit the teacher's input, and an LSTM is. Part 3 · The double penalty

A peak two hours late is punished twice

Why squared error charges a right-but-late peak twice, so it scores worse than no peak at all.  ·  Where high-resolution rain verification, flood-peak timing, Week 11’s power-spectrum checks.  ·  Do never rank fine-scale forecasts by squared error alone.

  • Squared error compares forecast and truth hour by hour, so a correct peak at the wrong time counts once as a miss and once as a false alarm. The only forecast that knows a peak is coming comes last.
  • Train on it and the model learns to hedge — smear the peak over every time it might arrive. In a river that is a shaved flood peak; in a weather map, a blurred rain band.
  • Persistence is a pure timing error — a k-day-late copy of the hydrograph — which is why it fell from 0.81 to −0.29 on our river.

Part 3 · The conditional mean

Squared error asks for the average of every possible future

Why blur is not a bug in the architecture; it is the correct answer to the question squared error asks.  ·  Where GraphCast’s day-10 maps, Week 4’s nowcasting U-Net, damped hydrographs.  ·  Do change the question (Part 4), not the backbone.

  • The c that minimizes E[(Y − c)²] is c = E[Y | what you know] — differentiate, set to zero, two lines on the board. A forecast trained on mean squared error (MSE) is pulled toward the conditional mean — that is what the loss rewards.
  • Early, the possible futures agree and their mean is sharp. Later they fan out and the mean flattens toward climatology. Measured: once the runs separate, a sharp run scores 1.96× the squared error of the blurred mean — squared error will always pick the blur.
conditional mean — the average of Y over every future consistent with the inputs. When those futures disagree, their average is smoother than any one of them. climatology — the long-run average state; the forecast you are left with when the inputs carry no more information about the future. Lorenz-63 — three coupled equations (Lorenz 1963), the smallest system that shows why weather has a predictability limit; here a stand-in for an atmosphere. in the figure — right: past the split, a sharp run’s error nears 2 × the climate variance and the mean’s 1 × — the arithmetic of two independent draws.

Part 3 · Hydrology’s version

NSE rewards a damped river: the best variability is r

Why hydrology’s own metric rewards shaving floods.  ·  Where every NSE-optimized model, including the one you train today.  ·  Do report KGE beside NSE, and look at the peaks.

  • Split NSE into its parts (Gupta et al. 2009): NSE = 2αr − α² − βn².
  • Hold the timing (r) fixed and maximize over the variability α: dNSE/dα = 2r − 2α = 0, so α* = r. A model with r = 0.8 scores its best NSE with 80% of the river’s real variability.
  • So maximizing NSE teaches a model to under-predict floods — the hydrologist’s name for blurring. Our river model 15 days out: r = 0.63, and its variability is 83% of the river’s.
  • KGE scores α on its own axis, so damping costs. The metric you train on is a choice of behaviour, not a formality.
r — correlation between simulated and observed flow: timing and shape. α — the ratio of their standard deviations: how big the swings are. βn — the bias, (mean simulated − mean observed) / σobserved. Set to 0 in the figure. in the figure — NSE for a fixed r = 0.8: the peak sits at α = 0.8, left of the honest α = 1. A model that shrinks every swing about the mean by 20% scores better.
Gupta, Kling, Yilmaz & Martinez 2009, Decomposition of the mean squared error and NSE performance criteria, J. Hydrol. 377 · derivation: Appendix A4

Part 4 · Heads

Ask the network for a distribution, not a number

Why one number must hedge; a distribution need not.  ·  Where Google Flood Hub’s distribution head, GenCast and AIFS-ENS ensembles, the W12 lab’s CRPS mini-ensemble.  ·  Do change one layer and one loss.

  • Same network, different last layer. A quantile head emits one number per probability level τ; a Gaussian head emits a mean μ and a spread σ; a mixture head emits several of those with weights.
  • The Gaussian head trains on the negative log-likelihood, L = log σ + (y − μ)² / 2σ². Where errors are large the cheapest way to lower L is a larger σ — the model learns how uncertain it should be. Keep σ positive with a softplus.
  • Why bother: decisions are thresholds — the chance that tomorrow’s flow exceeds bankfull — and a mean cannot answer that.
τ “tau” — the probability level of a quantile: q0.95 is the value the flow stays below 95% of the time. NLL “negative log-likelihood” — minus the log of the probability density the model gave to what actually happened. Low when the truth sat where the model put its mass. softplus — log(1 + eˣ): a smooth, always-positive transform for the σ output. in the figure — the same hidden state read out three ways: one spike, three ticks, one bell. Only the last layer and the loss differ.
Klotz et al. 2022, Uncertainty estimation with deep learning for rainfall–runoff modeling, HESS — the mixture heads compared on CAMELS · derivations: Appendix A9
3 min. Make it concrete for the lab: the change is one output layer and one loss function, nothing else in the LSTM moves. Derive the Gaussian NLL's σ behaviour in words, not on the board: for a fixed error, L is minimized at σ equal to the error's size, so the head learns the spread of the errors it makes — a heteroscedastic noise model for free. The warning for the lab: an unconstrained σ collapses toward zero on the training set and explodes the loss on the test set; softplus plus a small floor. Part 4 · The pinball loss

The pinball loss — and a band you can check

Why the pinball loss learns a quantile with no distributional assumption.  ·  Where ECMWF’s AIFS-ENS trains on a CRPS-based loss — the pinball loss integrated over τ; flood and energy forecasting.  ·  Do report coverage with every band.

  • ρτ(u) = max(τu, (τ − 1)u), u = y − q: under-prediction costs τ per unit, over-prediction 1 − τ. Its minimizer is the τ-quantile.
  • Measured, one day ahead: the 5–95% band caught 91.0% of 3,650 test days, and is 3.7 mm/day wide on high-flow days against 0.6 on low-flow days — it learned that a swollen river is less certain.
  • Integrate the pinball loss over every τ, times two, and you get the CRPS — Week 11’s score.

Appendix A1 · Backpropagation through time

Why the product vanishes, in full

ht = tanh(at),  at = W ht−1 + U xt + b  ·  step Jacobian: ∂hk/∂hk−1 = diag(1 − hk²) W
Error at step t collects every later loss: ∂L/∂ht = ΣT ≥ t (∂LT/∂hT) · ∏k=t+1..T diag(1 − hk²) W
Bound: ‖∏ diag(1 − hk²) W‖ ≤ (γ‖W‖)T−t, γ = max|tanh′| ≤ 1 — geometric decay whenever ‖W‖ < 1/γ
Shared weights sum over time: ∂L/∂W = Σt diag(1 − ht²) (∂L/∂ht) ht−1⊤ — Week 4’s shared filter, along the other axis
  • Truncated BPTT. Backpropagate only through a window of k steps. It caps memory and compute, and it also caps the dependency length gradients can teach — Kratzert’s 270-day input windows are the finite-window cousin of this.
  • Exploding. Clip the global norm, g ← g · min(1, c/‖g‖), the Week 2 safety net. Vanishing. No clipping helps; change the path — the LSTM’s additive lane, or next week’s attention, which connects every step to every other directly.
  • The bound is sufficient, not necessary: a large ‖W‖ does not guarantee explosion, because saturated tanh units have slopes near 0. That is why real RNNs mostly vanish.
Bengio, Simard & Frasconi 1994 · Pascanu, Mikolov & Bengio 2013, On the difficulty of training recurrent neural networks, ICML · Goodfellow Ch. 10, §10.2.2 and §10.7
Not taught in class. This is the derivation behind the 0.9¹⁵⁰ line of slide 6. Students preparing for the Book Check should be able to reproduce the step Jacobian and the norm bound from Goodfellow §10.7 without notes.

Appendix A2 · LSTM and GRU

The full equations, and what they cost

LSTM   ft, it, ot = σ(Wf,i,o[ht−1, xt] + bf,i,o)  ·  gt = tanh(Wg[ht−1, xt] + bg)
     ct = ft ⊙ ct−1 + it ⊙ gt  ·  ht = ot ⊙ tanh(ct)
GRU (biases omitted)   zt, rt = σ(Wz,r[ht−1, xt])  ·  h̃t = tanh(Wh[rt ⊙ ht−1, xt])  ·  ht = (1 − zt) ⊙ ht−1 + zt ⊙ h̃t
Parameters   LSTM 4·H·(H + D + 1)  ·  GRU 3·H·(H + D + 1)  H hidden units, D inputs
  • A lab-sized example. H = 64 and D = 6 (five weather variables plus yesterday’s flow): 4·64·71 = 18,176 weights, plus a 65-weight linear head. PyTorch keeps two bias vectors, so it reports 4·H·(H + D + 2) = 18,432. Kratzert et al. 2019 used H = 256.
  • Initialize the forget-gate bias high so memory is kept by default at the start of training (Gers et al. 2000; Jozefowicz et al. 2015). NeuralHydrology exposes it as initial_forget_bias.
  • The GRU’s update gate z interpolates between the old state and a new one — the additive path again, with one gate fewer. The unit is Cho et al. 2014; the name GRU is Chung et al. 2014.
Hochreiter & Schmidhuber 1997, Neural Computation 9 · Gers, Schmidhuber & Cummins 2000, Neural Computation 12 · Cho et al. 2014 · Chung et al. 2014 · Goodfellow Ch. 10, §10.10
Not taught in class. The parameter count is worth doing once by hand for the lab model — it is small enough that the LSTM is never the memory problem; the activations of a 270-day window across a batch are.

The EA-LSTM and the NSE* loss, as the paper writes them

i = σ(Wi xs + bi)  — static attributes xs only: computed once per basin, constant in time (Eq. 7)
ft, ot = σ(W dt + U ht−1 + b),  gt = tanh(Wg dt + Ug ht−1 + bg)  — dynamic inputs dt only

Appendix A4 · The metrics, derived

NSE, KGE and their benchmarks, in full

MSE = σs² + σo² − 2σsσor + (μs − μo)²  ⇒  NSE = 1 − MSE/σo² = 2αr − α² − βn²
α = σs/σo,  βn = (μs − μo)/σo  ·  ∂NSE/∂α = 2r − 2α = 0  ⇒  α* = r: damped whenever r < 1
KGE = 1 − √[(r − 1)² + (α − 1)² + (β − 1)²], β = μs/μo — each part optimal at 1, independently
Persistence at lead k is a shifted copy (α ≈ β ≈ 1 over a long, stationary record):  NSE = 2ρk − 1,  KGE = ρk, ρk the lag-k autocorrelation
  • The identity explains slide 9. CAMELS median persistence NSE 0.475 ⇒ ρ₁ ≈ 0.74, and the measured median KGE is 0.738. Our river: NSE 0.81 ⇒ ρ₁ ≈ 0.91.
  • Constant forecasts. The mean flow scores NSE = 0 by construction. Any constant scores KGE = 1 − √(2 + (β − 1)²) ≤ 1 − √2 ≈ −0.41, taking r = 0 because a constant has no correlation (Knoben et al. 2019).
  • NSE squares errors, so it is dominated by high flows; low-flow studies report NSE on log flows. Say which in the note.
Nash & Sutcliffe 1970, J. Hydrol. 10 · Gupta, Kling, Yilmaz & Martinez 2009, J. Hydrol. 377, Eqs. 4 and 9 · Knoben, Freer & Woods 2019, HESS 23:4323
Not taught in class. The persistence identity is the most useful line for the lab: once you know a basin's lag-1 autocorrelation you know its persistence NSE and KGE without running anything, and the two numbers on slide 9 stop looking like a paradox.

Appendix A6 · The error recursion, in full

A vector state, a Jacobian, and chaos

Vector state: ek+1 = Jk ek + δk+1, Jk = ∂f/∂y along the forecast  ⇒  ek = Σj (Jk−1 ⋯ Jj) δj
Covariance: Pk+1 = J Pk J⊤ + Q — the forecast step of a Kalman filter (Week 3’s data assimilation)
Constant J with spectral radius ρ(J) < 1: P settles at the solution of P = J P J⊤ + Q (a discrete Lyapunov equation)
Scalar case: Var(ek) = σ² (1 − a2k) / (1 − a²) → σ² / (1 − a²)  ·  river model: a = 0.911 → 5.9 σ²
  • Model error vs initial error. The recursion above is a model feeding on itself. A chaotic atmosphere adds a second source: even a perfect model grows a tiny initial error as eλt, λ the largest Lyapunov exponent.
  • Lorenz-63 as a check. λ ≈ 0.91 per time unit, so a 10⁻³ difference needs about ln(10³)/0.91 ≈ 7.6 units to reach order one — the runs of slide 22 split at about 6.5–7.
  • Correlated one-step errors change the saturation level but not the three regimes. Rollout training attacks model-error amplification; ensembles attack initial-condition uncertainty. Weather needs both.
Lorenz 1963 · Kalnay 2003, Atmospheric Modeling, Data Assimilation and Predictability, Ch. 6 · the scalar case is slide 15
Not taught in class. The Kalman-filter line is the bridge back to Week 3: the forecast step of data assimilation is exactly this covariance recursion, which is why operational centres think about forecast error the way this card does.

Appendix A7 · Rollout training

The rollout loss and its cheaper relatives, in code

# multi-step loss: backprop through K steps
y_hat, loss = y[t], 0
for k in range(1, K + 1):
    y_hat = model(y_hat, x[t + k])
    loss = loss + mse(y_hat, y[t + k])
(loss / K).backward()

# pushforward: unroll 2, backprop the last only
with torch.no_grad():
    y1 = model(y[t], x[t + 1])
loss = mse(model(y1, x[t + 2]), y[t + 2])
  • Multi-step loss — sees a directly; memory and compute grow with K. Gradient checkpointing (Week 12) buys the memory back with compute.
  • Pushforward trick (Brandstetter, Worrall & Welling 2022) — the second step trains on an input that carries the model’s own error, at the memory cost of one step.
  • Scheduled sampling (Bengio et al. 2015) — at each step feed the truth with probability ε, the model’s own output otherwise, and decay ε during training.
  • Input-noise injection — corrupt training inputs with noise shaped like rollout error, so f is trained in a neighbourhood of the data rather than on it; learned physics simulators do this routinely.
Brandstetter, Worrall & Welling 2022, Message passing neural PDE solvers, ICLR, §3.1 · Bengio, Vinyals, Jaitly & Shazeer 2015, NeurIPS · Sanchez-Gonzalez et al. 2020, ICML (input noise)
Not taught in class; the crib for discussion question 2's "noise injection". The unifying sentence: every one of these shows the model, during training, inputs that look like its own mistakes — they differ only in how faithfully and at what cost.

Appendix A8 · The weather models

How four weather models handle their own rollout

  • GraphCast (Lam et al. 2023) — 6-hour step, ERA5 1979–2017. Phase 1: 1,000 updates at 1 step, learning rate 0 → 10⁻³. Phase 2: 299,000 updates at 1 step, half-cosine decay to 0. Phase 3: 11,000 updates, rollout 2 → 12 steps (+1 every 1,000), learning rate fixed at 3 × 10⁻⁷. Loss: MSE weighted by latitude-cell area and by pressure level (weights ∝ pressure); at the surface, 2-m temperature 1.0 and the others 0.1.
  • Pangu-Weather (Bi et al. 2023) — four models with 1-, 3-, 6- and 24-hour steps; a greedy rule “always calls for the deep network with the largest affordable lead time” — 56 h = 24 × 2 + 6 + 1 × 2 — “to reduce the cumulative forecast errors”. Mean-absolute-error loss; train 1979–2017, validate 2019, test 2018.
  • FuXi (Chen et al. 2023) — a cascade of three fine-tuned copies: FuXi-Short for 0–5 days, FuXi-Medium for 5–10, FuXi-Long for 10–15, handing over at steps 20 and 40.
  • FourCastNet (Pathak et al. 2022) — 80 epochs of one-step pretraining, then 50 epochs fine-tuning on a second, self-fed step with both losses summed.
  • An absolute-error loss is minimized by the conditional median rather than the mean — still one number, still a hedge between futures.
Lam et al. 2023, Science 382 (arXiv:2212.12794, Supp. 4.2 and 4.5) · Bi et al. 2023, Nature 619 · Chen et al. 2023, npj Clim. Atmos. Sci. · Pathak et al. 2022, arXiv:2202.11214
Not taught in class. Read it as four placements on the recursion of slide 15: GraphCast and FourCastNet shape a by training on the rollout; Pangu and FuXi reduce k or give each lead-time range its own model. None of them removes the blur — that needs Part 4's move to distributions.

Appendix A9 · Probabilistic losses

Quantile, likelihood and CRPS, derived

Pinball: d/dq E[ρτ(Y − q)] = −τ·P(Y > q) + (1 − τ)·P(Y < q) = 0  ⇒  P(Y < q*) = τ, so q* is the τ-quantile
Gaussian NLL: −log N(y; μ, σ²) = log σ + (y − μ)²/2σ² + ½ log 2π; for an error e, d/dσ = 1/σ − e²/σ³ = 0 ⇒ σ = |e|
CRPS(F, y) = ∫ (F(z) − 𝟙{z ≥ y})² dz = E|X − y| − ½ E|X − X′| = 2 ∫₀¹ ρτ(y − F⁻¹(τ)) dτ
Gaussian closed form: CRPS = σ [ z(2Φ(z) − 1) + 2φ(z) − 1/√π ], z = (y − μ)/σ
  • Ensembles. With members x₁ … xM: (1/M) Σ|xi − y| − (1/2M²) Σi,j|xi − xj|. The first term rewards accuracy, the second credits spread, so a blurred single forecast cannot win.
  • On CAMELS Klotz et al. 2022 compared a Gaussian mixture, CMAL, UMAL and Monte Carlo dropout; CMAL was best overall and dropout worst. Nearing et al. 2024 use one asymmetric Laplacian per lead time, trained on the NLL.
  • ECMWF’s machine-learning ensemble is trained with a CRPS-based loss — the score is becoming the objective. Week 11 builds verification on it.
Koenker & Bassett 1978 · Gneiting & Raftery 2007, JASA · Gneiting et al. 2005 (Gaussian CRPS) · Klotz et al. 2022, HESS 26 · CMAL and UMAL: countable and uncountable mixtures of asymmetric Laplacians
Not taught in class. The pinball derivation is two lines and worth doing once: it is why a quantile head needs no distributional assumption at all. The CRPS identity with the pinball loss is the bridge to Week 11 — a set of quantile heads was already estimating the CRPS.
