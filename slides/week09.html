Part 1 · Definition · 2 min

A foundation model: pretrain once on broad data, adapt to many tasks

The expensive part is paid once, by someone else; you pay only for the adaptation. That is the deal your capstone tests.

Prithvi-EO-2.0 · NASA–IBM300 M / 600 M · HLS time series · land tasksAurora · Microsoft1.3 B · weather, air quality, waves, cyclones
Prithvi-WxC · NASA–IBM2.3 B · MERRA-2 · downscaling, gravity wavesAlphaEarth · Google DeepMindyearly 64-number embedding per 10 m pixel
Definition: Bommasani et al. 2021, §1 — “any model that is trained on broad data … that can be adapted … to a wide range of downstream tasks”
2 min. The definition is Bommasani's: broad data, adaptation to many tasks; scale is what made it work, not what defines it. The picture is the economics: the left arrow costs weeks on tens of GPUs and is done once by a lab; the right arrows are what you do, with hundreds or thousands of labels. Read the four Earth models in one line each — two of them (Prithvi-EO-2.0 and Aurora) are today's archaeology examples. The capstone's question, in this picture: does the left arrow help your right arrow, compared with a model that never took the left arrow (the random-init control) and a strong model trained only on your labels (the supervised baseline)?

Part 1 · Chinchilla, the reading · 2.5 min

For a fixed budget there is a best size — grow data with it

Chinchilla (70 B parameters, 1.4 T tokens) beat Gopher (280 B, 300 B tokens) on the same compute: Gopher was too big for its data.

  • Double the compute → √2 × more parameters and √2 × more tokens; at the optimum, ≈ 20 tokens per parameter.
scaling law — loss falls as a smooth power of model size N and data D · compute-optimal — the N, D pair with the lowest loss for a budget C ≈ 6 N D FLOPs · token — one input unit: a word piece in text, a patch for us
Curves: L(N, D) = E + A / Nα + B / Dβ, Hoffmann et al. 2022 §3.3, constants refitted by Besiroglu et al. 2024 · fitted on text, not Earth data · tools/week09_deck/figs/fig_isoflop.py
2.5 min. Walk along the orange curve: same compute everywhere on it; left is a small model trained on a lot of data, right a big model on little. The bottom is the best split. In 2021 the field sat to the right — Gopher, 280 billion parameters on 300 billion tokens. Chinchilla spent the same compute on a model four times smaller with four times more data, and won on almost every benchmark. The rule that came out: parameters and tokens should grow together, each by the square root of the compute, which ends near 20 tokens per parameter. The blue curve is the point for us: a week of free GPU puts the best size around 100 million parameters — if you had 3 billion tokens of fresh data, which you do not; next slide. Honest caveat: these curves are fitted on text; the exponents for images and weather fields are different and measured far less often (A3).

Part 1 · What is a token of your data? · 2 min

Earth models are data-poor and repeat their corpus hundreds of times

Chinchilla’s 20 counts fresh text tokens. Earth archives hold few unique tokens, so their models loop over them.

1 · Fix N, vary tokens — at each FLOP budget keep the run with the lowest loss

2 · IsoFLOP — fix 9 budgets, vary N; the bottom of each U is the best size (slide 5)

3 · Fit a formula — L(N, D) = E + A / Nα + B / Dβ to every run, then minimise it

Where ≈ 20 comes from: 400+ language models, 70 M – 16 B; all three ways give N, D ∝ √C, so D / N ≈ 20. Earth data: grow D faster (TESSERA v2: D ∝ C0.63).

token — Prithvi: 16 × 16 px × 6 bands, one date · Aurora: 4 × 4 cells, all variables, one level · ours: 8 × 8 px × 13 bands
Muennighoff et al. 2023 · TESSERA v2 §3.3 · Prithvi-EO-2.0 §III · Aurora Supp. §C–D (counts derived, ≈) · figs/fig_tokens.py
2 min. This is the reading's first question — "what is a token of ERA5?". The answer: a patch, like an image. Read the dots. Chinchilla: 20 fresh text tokens per parameter, seen once. Prithvi-EO-2.0-300M: its 4.2 million four-date chips hold about 11 unique patch tokens per parameter — fewer than Chinchilla's rule — so it trains 400 epochs and sees each token 400 times. Aurora has more unique tokens per parameter (a global field every 6 hours adds up), but neighbouring hours are nearly identical, so "unique" overstates it. Our own mini-MAE had a quarter of a token per parameter and saw each one 1,367 times; it still learned useful features, because each pass hides a different 75% (masking acts like fresh data, partly). Then the three cards — where the 20 itself comes from: Hoffmann et al. trained more than 400 language models from 70 million to 16 billion parameters and asked, three ways, which N and D give the lowest loss for a fixed compute budget. One: for each model size, vary the number of training tokens and, at every FLOP budget, keep whichever run reached the lowest loss. Two: fix nine budgets (6 × 10^18 to 3 × 10^21 FLOPs), train several sizes at each, fit a parabola and read off the bottom — the U-curves of the last slide. Three: fit one loss formula to all runs and minimise it. All three say parameters and tokens should grow at the same rate, about the square root of the compute, so their ratio stays near 20 — Table 3: 1 billion parameters, 20 billion tokens; the paper never writes "20" itself. Two lessons for us. One, for text, repeating up to 4 epochs costs almost nothing and returns vanish after about 16 (Muennighoff). Two, the one EO study that fitted Chinchilla-style exponents found data should grow faster than the model — and that pretraining loss barely predicts downstream skill (|r| below 0.2), so judge pilots by a probe on your validation split, not by the loss curve. Reading question for ERA5: 40 years every 6 hours at Aurora's 4 × 4 patches is about 58,000 frames × 65,000 patches ≈ 4 billion tokens per level.

Part 1 · Worked budget · 3 min

Your contract in GPU-hours: adapting fits, pretraining does not

Every row of your Talk 1 contract has a price. Write it down now, then replace it with a measured step time on 23 Nov.

  • Cost ≈ 6 × parameters × tokens seen. The random-init control is the dearest row — and the one you may not drop.
Assumed: Prithvi-EO-2.0-300M encoder · 2,000 train + 500 validation chips, 224 × 224, one date → 196 tokens · fine-tune 30 epochs, scratch 100 · T4 at 30% of its 65 TFLOP/s (16-bit) · supervised U-Net 0.2 h a seed, a guess
Computed for this lecture: tools/week09_deck/figs/budget.py → budget.json · Kaggle ≈ 30 free GPU-hours a week (T4 / P100), Capstone page
3 min. This is the slide to work with one student's contract: ask whoever gave the clearest budget in Talk 1 for their model size, number of chips, tokens per chip and epochs, and redo the arithmetic on the board — 6 × N × tokens per chip × chips × epochs, divided by about 2 × 10^13 FLOP/s. With the default numbers: one fine-tune of a 300 M encoder on 2,000 chips for 30 epochs is about a third of a GPU-hour; the whole contract with three seeds, tuning, one ablation and a 30% restart reserve is about 11 hours — a third of one Kaggle week. The expensive row is the random-init control, because a model trained from scratch needs more epochs; do not drop it, it is what tells you whether pretraining mattered. The right panel is the point of the scaling law: pretraining the same encoder the Chinchilla way — 20 tokens per parameter, 6 billion tokens, about 31 million chips — would cost about 150 hours, five Kaggle weeks, and you do not have 31 million chips. So the capstone adapts a public model, and the mini-MAE pilots stay small. Caveats to say: 30% utilisation is an assumption, attention and the decoder head are left out (a few per cent for 196 tokens; more for long time series), and the T4's 16 GB of memory may force a small batch or LoRA (low-rank adaptation, Week 12) — measure on 23 November.

Part 2 · Example 1 · Prithvi-EO-2.0 §III-A · 2.5 min

Prithvi-EO-2.0: a sampled, filtered and split corpus — read each step

Every arrow is a choice that decides what the model knows. Your audit copies each one, with its section number.

  • Bands B02–B07 (blue, green, red, near-infrared, two short-wave infrared): feed exactly these, in this order, with its mean and std from the model’s config.
stratify — sample so that every land-cover class and ecoregion is represented · Fmask — the HLS quality band that flags cloud and cloud shadow
Szwarcman et al., Prithvi-EO-2.0, arXiv 2412.02732v3 §III-A–B · Hugging Face config ibm-nasa-geospatial/Prithvi-EO-2.0-300M · course-design/wf_week9-facts.md §7
2.5 min. Walk the five boxes. One, the archive is every HLS land tile. Two, they did not take all of it: they picked tiles so that every land-cover class and almost every ecoregion appears, pushed urban up and sea and desert down — so the model has seen more cities and farmland than the Earth has. Three, the split is by tile and happens before cutting chips; that is the blocked split you committed in Talk 1. Four, each sample is four dates, one to six months apart, from 2014 to 2023, six bands, 30 m. Five, cloudy or incomplete chips are thrown away — so the model has rarely seen a cloud; if your task has clouds, that is a mismatch you should name. And they dropped training chips that overlapped validation areas. The orange line is the archaeology finding: the paper does not say whether benchmark areas were excluded. For your project that means: if your test region and years fall inside 2014–2023 HLS land, you cannot rule out that the encoder saw them; say so, and prefer a test period after 2023 where you can. Cost line: 21,000 A100-hours for the 300 M model — 700 times your Kaggle week.

Part 2 · Example 2 · Aurora, Supplementary §C · 2 min

Aurora: ten datasets, all ending in 2020 — so test on 2022 and later

For a weather model the clean split is by time. Read where the corpus ends before you choose your test years.

  • Normalised with ERA5 means and stds for every dataset; ERA5 loss weighted × 2. Note: the 2020 validation year sits inside the pretraining years.
reforecast — an old forecast re-run with today’s model · CMIP6 — the climate-model comparison project; here, simulated weather, not observations
Bodnar et al., Nature 2025, Supplementary Table C3 and §C.4 (arXiv 2405.13063v3 §11) · 1.3 B parameters, 150 k steps on 32 A100s ≈ 2.5 weeks · weights MIT · wf_week9-facts.md §8
2 min. The section is in the supplement, Section C — not Appendix B, as our schedule said. Ten datasets, 1.3 petabytes: reanalyses, forecasts, ensembles, even climate simulations. Three things to read off. First, everything ends in 2020; the test years are 2022 and 2023 — a clean split by time, and the one you should copy if your project is about weather or climate: put your test years after the corpus ends. Second, normalisation: Aurora uses ERA5's means and standard deviations for every dataset; when you fine-tune, keep those statistics rather than recomputing your own. Third, a small honesty check: the validation year 2020 is inside the pretraining datasets' range — the kind of detail the audit should record, not hide. They also found that adding every dataset was not always better than ERA5 alone: more data is not automatically better data.

Part 2 · The overlap check · 2 min

Did your base model already see your test data?

If it did, the frozen probe and the fine-tune look better than they would on new places or years. Decide now, before any run.

  • BASE_MODEL_AUDIT.md, first lines: model + exact revision · corpus sensors / variables · places and dates · filters and normalisation · held-out data and overlap with your test · licence.
exact revision — the Hugging Face commit hash of the weights you load, so a silent update cannot change your results
Template: Appendix A4 · it goes in your repo by Sun 15 Nov and is card 2 of Talk 2
2 min. Three cases. Known no overlap — Aurora with a test year of 2022 or later: test as planned, and cite the section. Known overlap — for example your test region and years are inside ERA5 1979–2020 and you use Aurora: move the test block, and amend PREREG.md with a date, before the run. Unknown — the common case, Prithvi's benchmark areas: you cannot prove a negative, so write UNVERIFIED, add a test block after the corpus ends (2024 or later for Prithvi-EO-2.0), and report the frozen probe on both blocks; if the possibly-seen block scores clearly higher, that is evidence the encoder remembered it. Then the six audit lines: read them once; A4 is the template, and you start it in the studio today.

Part 2 · When does pretraining pay? · 2 min

With many labels a plain U-Net often wins; with few, pretraining starts to pay

That is why your contract has a supervised baseline that tries to win — and why your label count decides the result.

  • Cheap ablation for your contract: rerun the four rows at 10% of your labels — the gap that appears there is the case for pretraining.
PANGAEA — an Earth-model benchmark of 11 datasets; frozen encoders + a UPerNet decoder vs a U-Net and a ViT trained from scratch · CROMA — a radar + optical pretrained model
Marsocci et al. 2025, PANGAEA, arXiv 2412.04204v2 §5.1–5.2 · GEO-Bench-2, arXiv 2511.15658, Abstract · Feng et al. 2026, TESSERA v2, Abstract · wf_week9-facts.md §6, §9
2 min. PANGAEA froze each foundation model's encoder, put the same decoder on it, and compared with a U-Net trained from scratch. With all labels, the U-Net is in the top two on six datasets — more than any foundation model; it is best on burn scars, floods and small farms. Cut the labels to 10% and the U-Net drops to two, while CROMA reaches six. GEO-Bench-2 says the same in other words: no model wins everywhere. TESSERA adds a warning for your pilots: a lower pretraining loss does not mean a better downstream model. For the capstone: your supervised baseline must be tuned and allowed to win; and if you have time for one ablation, the label fraction is the most informative — it shows where, if anywhere, pretraining pays. Caveat: PANGAEA's Prithvi was the 1.0 era; Prithvi-EO-2.0 was not in that table.

Appendix A2 · Counting compute

A2 · GPU-hours = 6 · N · tokens ÷ (peak × utilisation × 3600)

  • 6 N per token: forward 2 N (one multiply + one add per weight), backward 4 N (PaLM App. B). A frozen probe needs only the forward pass, once: 2 N per token.
  • Attention adds ≈ 4 · T² · d per layer. ViT-L (24 layers, d 1024): +3% at T = 196 tokens, +13% at T = 784 (Prithvi’s four dates). Long time series: count it.
  • MAE pretraining: the encoder sees 25% of the tokens, so its cost is ≈ ¼; add the decoder (Week 7, A5).
  • Utilisation: real runs reach a fraction of peak — GPT-3 21%, Gopher 33%, PaLM 46% (PaLM Table 3, model-FLOPs utilisation). We assumed 30% on a T4 (65 TFLOP/s at 16 bits); a P100 has no tensor cores (18.7 TFLOP/s).
  • Then measure: time 50 steps, multiply out. That number replaces this formula in Talk 3.
PaLM, arXiv 2204.02311 §4.1, App. B · NVIDIA T4 and P100 data sheets · Kaggle: “30 hours or sometimes higher” a week, 12-hour sessions · Colab publishes no limits · wf_week9-facts.md §11
Reference only.

Appendix A3 · Data-limited scaling, and Earth-data evidence

A3 · When the corpus is fixed: repeat it, and judge by the downstream task

  • Muennighoff et al. 2023 (text): “up to 4 epochs of repeated data yields negligible changes to loss compared to having unique data”; gains fade after ≈ 16 epochs.
  • Aurora: at a fixed 5 k GPU-hours, validation loss ∝ N−0.026 — ≈ 6% better per 10× parameters (Supp. §G). Adding every dataset was not always better than ERA5 alone.
  • Wickrema et al. 2025: over a quadrillion pixels, “performance is consistent with a data-limited regime”.
  • TESSERA v2 (2026): best encoder ∝ C0.36, data ∝ C0.63; pretraining loss vs downstream skill |r| < 0.2.
  • PhilEO scaling (2025): a 200 M U-Net beats larger models on regression in low-shot settings.
arXiv 2305.16264 · Aurora, Nature 2025 Supp. §G · arXiv 2512.23903 · arXiv 2607.03949 §3.2–3.3 · arXiv 2506.14765 · wf_week9-facts.md §4, §6
Reference only. Answers reading question 2 (why exponents differ for imagery): redundancy between neighbouring patches and dates, repeated epochs, masking as augmentation, and the weak link between loss and downstream skill.
