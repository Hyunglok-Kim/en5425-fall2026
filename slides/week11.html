Part 1 · this week’s paper

How a Data-Driven Weather Model Works

What you will see
Three slides: the forecast as one learned six-hour step fed back into itself; three ways to put a sphere into a network; three ways to get fifty forecasts instead of one. By the end: place any weather model you read about on these three axes.

Why it matters here

Where it is used

Part 1 · The forecasting problem

A forecast is one learned six-hour step, fed back into itself

The network learns the step from ERA5; the state it starts from still comes from a numerical weather system.

  • The baseline is hard to beat: ECMWF integrates the equations of the atmosphere at about 9 km to 15 days — one run from its best analysis plus fifty perturbed ones.
  • The networks are small: tens of millions of weights, trained in hours to weeks on 8 to 192 GPUs; one forecast then takes about a minute.
HRES — ECMWF’s single 9 km forecast · ENS — its ensemble of 51 runs · GPU — graphics processor
Lam et al. 2023, GraphCast (the reading) · the numerical baseline: Bauer et al. 2015 · when each model went operational, and ECMWF’s product names: A1 · five models in one table: A2
1.8 min. Read the picture left to right. The state is every variable on every level and grid point at one moment: GraphCast uses five variables at the surface and six on each of 37 pressure levels, 227 fields on a quarter-degree grid, about a million points each. The model takes the state now and six hours ago and returns the state six hours later; feed that back in forty times and you have ten days — exactly the autoregressive rollout of Week 5, with the same error growth. Then the orange box, the two limits to keep in mind all hour. One: the network does not start itself. Every operational data-driven forecast begins from the analysis of a numerical system, which turns observations into a state; the learned model replaces the forecast step, not the assimilation. Two: it does not judge itself. Almost all of these models learned from ERA5, a reanalysis made with a 2016 model at about 31 km; a score against ERA5 measures how well the network imitates ERA5, and the numerical forecast it is compared with was never built to do that. First bullet: the competitor. ECMWF's system has 137 levels and about 9 km spacing; one run from the best analysis — called HRES until recently — and fifty more with perturbed starts and physics; together the ensemble, ENS. Second bullet: the scale. GraphCast has 36.7 million weights, smaller than last week's Prithvi; training took 32 accelerators four weeks, and a ten-day forecast takes under a minute on one, against about an hour on 11,664 processor cores for the numerical run.

Part 1 · Architecture · this week’s paper is the middle one

Three ways to put a sphere into a network: windows, a mesh, harmonics

The latitude–longitude grid is the problem: its cells shrink toward the poles, so equal windows cover unequal areas.

  • GraphCast: 40,962 mesh nodes, 16 rounds of messages; a long edge crosses a continent in one step.
  • Architecture is not what decides: Stormer, a plain transformer at 1.4°, leads beyond day 7 in its authors’ tests.
message passing — each node updates from its neighbours · spherical harmonics — the sphere’s own Fourier basis
Bi et al. 2023 (Pangu-Weather) · Lam et al. 2023 (GraphCast) · Bonev et al. 2023 (spherical Fourier neural operator) · Nguyen et al. 2024 (Stormer) · the mesh, drawn: A3
1.5 min. Every design starts from one difficulty. On the latitude–longitude grid a window of a fixed number of points is more than a thousand kilometres wide at the equator and a few tens of kilometres near the pole. Family (a) keeps that grid, cuts it into patches and uses attention inside windows — the Swin idea of Week 6, here in three dimensions: Pangu-Weather, FuXi, Aurora. Family (b), today's reading, leaves the grid for the computation: GraphCast maps the gridded state onto the nodes of an icosahedron refined six times — 40,962 nodes, nearly equally spaced — lets the nodes exchange messages along the edges sixteen times, and maps back. The mesh keeps the edges of every refinement level, so a message can cross a continent along a long edge in one step and be refined along the short ones. ECMWF's AIFS uses a graph to go between the grid and a coarser internal representation, and a transformer in between. Family (c) computes on spectral coefficients; with spherical harmonics no point is special, and its authors report stable six-hour rollouts for a year. Second bullet, the sober one: Stormer is an ordinary vision transformer on a 1.4-degree grid, trained with rollouts that grow and a step drawn at random from 6, 12 or 24 hours; its authors report it ahead beyond seven days of the models they compared, some at five times its resolution. What you train on, and how, matters more than which of the three families you pick.

Part 1 · Probabilistic models

One forecast is the average of many futures — three ways to get fifty instead

Trained on squared error, a model answers uncertainty with the mean: right on average, smooth, and no storm in it.

  • A deterministic model already behaves like an ensemble mean: it beats ECMWF’s ensemble mean only for the first three to six days.
diffusion model — a network that turns random noise into a sample, a little at each step · proper score — a score that only an honest forecast distribution minimises; the CRPS is one
Price et al. 2025 (GenCast) · Lang et al. 2024 (AIFS-CRPS) · Alet et al. 2025 (FGN) · Rasp et al. 2024 (WeatherBench 2) · the three routes drawn with fields: A4
1.5 min. Week 5 derived it for a river: a model trained on squared error predicts the conditional mean. Beyond a few days the atmosphere could be in many states, and the mean of many states is smoother than any of them — a good score, a poor description of the weather that will happen. To say how likely a storm is, a forecast must show several possible futures. Three routes; orange marks what is random. One: generate from noise. GenCast is a diffusion model: given the two previous states it refines random noise in twenty steps into the state twelve hours later; different noise, different member. Fifty members beat ECMWF's ensemble on 97.2% of 1,320 targets in 2019; the price is eight minutes per fifteen-day member, against under a minute for a deterministic model of that size. Two: give the network a random vector as an extra input and train it directly on the CRPS, which is lowest when the spread of the members matches their error. One forward pass per step. This is ECMWF's operational ensemble, AIFS-CRPS, and Google's FGN, which draws 32 numbers per member and step. Three: perturb the initial state and run a deterministic model from each — cheapest, but every member is still a smoothed forecast, and smooth forecasts from nearby starts stay close. We test routes like this one on our emulator in Part 2. The bullet: in WeatherBench 2 the deterministic data-driven models beat the numerical ensemble mean only in the first three to six days; they are means without an ensemble.

Part 2 · Skill against lead time

An hour of training gives five useful days — judged against two forecasts that cost nothing

A score means little alone: put it beside the forecast anyone could make without a model.

  • Useful for 5.2 days; persistence, 1.5. ECMWF’s numerical forecast: 8 to 9 days, on a grid 70 times finer.
  • Past day 6.3 climatology has the lower error, yet ACC is still above 0.4: the double penalty of Week 5.
RMSE — root-mean-square error, each grid row weighted by its area · ACC — correlation between forecast and observed departures from climatology; “useful above 0.6” is a convention from 1978
Measured for this course: model A, 1,440 forecasts from 00 and 12 UTC, 2018–2019, against ERA5 · scores as in WeatherBench 2 · code: A9
1.6 min. The first thing to draw for any forecast model, and for your project: skill against lead time, next to the forecasts that cost nothing. Left panel, error. Grey is persistence — tomorrow equals today — which is good for a few hours and useless after two days. The dashed line is climatology, the average for that date: it never gets worse, so it is the ceiling a forecast has to stay under. Orange is model A: 147 after one day, where persistence has 570; 673 at day five. Right panel, the same forecasts scored by pattern: the anomaly correlation asks whether the highs and lows are in the right places. The convention since a 1978 ECMWF report is that a forecast is useful while this is above 0.6. Persistence: a day and a half. Our emulator: 5.2 days — from one hour of training on a grid of 2,048 cells. ECMWF's numerical forecast reaches eight to nine days at about seventy times our resolution, and the large data-driven models a little more. Second bullet, the subtle one: after 6.3 days the emulator's error is above climatology's, and yet the correlation is still above 0.4 — the patterns are there, slightly displaced, and squared error charges a displaced pattern twice, once where it is and once where it should be. That was Week 5's double penalty. So read both panels: the error says when to stop using the numbers, the correlation says the forecast still knows something. In your project: what is your persistence, and what is your climatology? If you cannot name them, Talk 4's table has no floor.

Part 2 · Blur

The forecast fades before it fails: big features stay in place, small ones vanish

Squared error rewards the average of the possible futures, and an average is smoother than any one of them.

  • One forecast, ERA5 above, model B below: ACC falls 0.98 → 0.88 → 0.54; the sharp features weaken or move.
  • All 1,440: the forecast keeps 76% of the smallest waves’ power after one step, 62% after a day.
anomaly — the departure from climatology for that date · power — the variance a field carries at one wavelength
Measured for this course · power: model A, wavenumbers 21 to 32 (900–1,350 km at 45° N), relative to ERA5 · the same loss in the large models: Rasp et al. 2024 · why: Week 5
1.5 min. One forecast, so you can see what the curves mean. Top row is ERA5 one, three and five days after the start on 18 December 2018; the colours are the departure of the 500-hectopascal surface from its climatology, in metres. Bottom row is model B. After one day you cannot tell them apart: correlation 0.98. After three days the big highs and lows are in the right places, a little weak: 0.88. After five days — just under the 0.6 line — look at what is left: the large pattern is roughly right, and what was small or strong is weaker, displaced or gone. That is not a failure of this small network; it is what squared error asks for. When several futures are possible, the answer that minimises squared error is their average, and an average of maps is smoother than any of them: Week 5's conditional mean. Second bullet: the same thing measured over all 1,440 forecasts. Take the shortest waves this grid can hold — 900 to 1,350 kilometres — and ask how much of ERA5's variance at those scales the forecast still has: 76% after a single six-hour step, 62% after a day. The smoothing starts at the first step, before any error has accumulated. WeatherBench 2 reports the same for GraphCast and Pangu-Weather in their first days. For your project: a model that scores well on squared error can still be missing the peak, the edge or the flood — look at a map, not only at the number.

Part 2 · Training on its own output

Rollout training buys 8% at day 3 — and pays part of it in blur

Trained on its own output, the model learns to hedge: lower error later, smoother fields throughout.

  • A quarter more training, on rollouts of 2 then 4 steps: day-3 error −8%, useful range 5.2 → 5.6 days.
  • The price, on the right: at day 5 it keeps 48% of the smallest waves’ power; the one-step model, 64%.
fine-tuning — training on from learned weights · wavenumber — waves around a latitude circle; high = small features
Models A and B, 1,440 forecasts · seeds of B differ by under 1% · GraphCast: rollouts of 2 to 12 steps (Lam et al. 2023) · is 8% more than noise? slide 18
1.5 min. Model A never saw its own output in training; model B did, for 422 more updates — rollouts of two steps, then four, with the error summed over the steps. Left panel: the change in error from A to B. At six hours nothing changes, as it should: the first step was already trained. Then the gain grows, to 8% for the geopotential near day 3 and 6% for temperature, and shrinks again as both models approach climatology. The useful range moves from 5.2 to 5.6 days. A quarter more training, on the thing the model is used for. Every large model does this: GraphCast lengthens its rollouts from two to twelve steps at the end of training, FuXi and AIFS similarly; Pangu-Weather avoids long rollouts another way, with separate models for 1, 3, 6 and 24 hours. Right panel, the price. This is the day-5 forecast's variance divided by ERA5's, wave by wave; 1 means as much detail as the real atmosphere. Both models lose the small scales, and the fine-tuned one loses more: 48% against 64% in the shaded band. Part of the 8% is real — the model has learned to correct its own errors — and part is hedging: a smoother forecast is punished less. You cannot tell which from the error alone; you need the second diagnostic. That is the general lesson: when a training trick lowers your error, ask what it did to the thing the error does not see. And is 8% more than seed noise? Five seeds of B differ by less than 1%; slide 18 puts an interval on it.

Part 2 · Five seeds

Five seeds make an ensemble for free — and it is too sure of itself

The same recipe trained five times from different random starts: the cheapest ensemble there is.

  • The mean of five beats each member: error 568 against 631 at day 5, useful for 6.2 days instead of 5.6.
  • But the members agree with each other more than with the truth: spread is half the error, and ERA5 falls outside all five in 58% of cases; a reliable ensemble gives 33%.
spread — the members’ standard deviation · rank histogram — the truth’s rank among the members; flat if reliable
Measured for this course: five copies of model B, 1,440 forecasts · spread corrected for the five members · a perturbed-start ensemble does no better (A4) · code: A9
1.6 min. You will each train at least three seeds per row. Here is what five seeds of model B are worth. Left panel: grey is the error of one model, solid orange the error of the average of the five — lower at every lead time, 568 against 631 at day five, and useful for 6.2 days instead of 5.6. Averaging cancels the part of each model's error that is its own. That is free skill, and it is why a seed average is a fair row in a table only if every row gets one. The dashed line is the catch: the spread of the five, how far they lie apart. In a reliable ensemble the truth behaves like one more member, so spread and error are about equal. Here spread is about half the error — 0.43 after six hours, 0.55 after three days. Middle panel, the same statement as a histogram: sort the five members at every grid point and ask where ERA5 falls. If the ensemble were reliable, each of the six positions would hold a sixth of the cases. Instead ERA5 is below all five 26% of the time and above all five 32%: 58% outside, where 33% is expected. Five networks trained on the same data with the same loss make the same mistakes. Right panel: even so, scored as a probability forecast the five beat one — a CRPS of 167 against a mean absolute error of 246 at day three. So: seeds give you a better mean and a lower bound on your uncertainty, not the uncertainty. The models of slide 6 exist because of this gap: their spread is trained to match the error. For Talk 4: report the seed mean and the seed spread, and do not call the spread an error bar on the truth.

1 · Which product?
The 9 km forecast, its coarser twin from the ensemble, or the ensemble mean — the mean is the hardest to beat after a week.

2 · Scored against what?
ERA5 is what the network learned to imitate; a numerical forecast was built to match its own analysis. Fair: each against its own.

3 · Started from what?
ERA5 at 00 and 12 UTC has seen observations up to 9 hours ahead; a real-time analysis, 3. Worth up to 5% of GraphCast’s error.

2 min. Every one of these papers has a sentence saying the model is more accurate than ECMWF's forecast. Read the table across. Pangu-Weather: compared with the control forecast taken from a public archive — the unperturbed member of the ensemble, which then ran at 18 km, half the resolution of the high-resolution forecast — and both were scored against ERA5; at day five, 296.7 against 333.7. GraphCast: compared with the 9 km forecast itself, and scored fairly — GraphCast against ERA5, the numerical forecast against its own analysis; ahead on 90.3% of 1,380 combinations of variable, level and lead time, and on 99.7% once the two stratospheric levels, 50 and 100 hectopascals, are left out. Together this is strong evidence — independent groups, different architectures, the same result — and it is evidence about one kind of error: smooth, large-scale fields on a coarsened grid, which is what the models were trained to minimise. The three cards are the questions that make the four rows comparable. One: which product. Two: scored against what — a model trained on ERA5 and scored on ERA5 is being scored on its own training target. Three: started from what — ERA5's analysis at 00 and 12 UTC used observations up to nine hours after that time, an operational analysis three; GraphCast's authors measured up to 5% lower error from those starts and therefore evaluated from 06 and 18 UTC. Now turn it round. Next Monday you write "the foundation model is better than the supervised baseline". Which baseline, tuned how hard? Scored against which labels? Given which inputs — did one row see something the other could not? Same three questions.

Part 3 · What the scores leave out

Good on average, weak at the extremes

Smooth fields have low peaks, and a network cannot forecast a kind of weather it has never seen.

  • Detail: Pangu-Weather’s fields show no realistic variability below 500 to 700 km; the numerical model’s, about 200 km — both are issued on a 0.25° grid or finer.
  • Never seen: on record-breaking heat, cold and wind the numerical forecast stays ahead; trained without the strongest cyclones, a model could not produce one.

Part 3 · The interval on a difference

Paired block bootstrap: the interval that belongs on every difference you report

A difference without an interval is one draw of the dice; resample your test cases to see how far it could move.

  • Block — the unit that is independent in your data: a 10-day window, a basin, a tile, a flood event. Neighbouring samples move together; drawn one by one they give an interval that is too narrow.
  • Paired — one draw for every row of your table: a hard case is hard for all your models, and that shared part cancels in the difference. Write “better” only when the interval excludes zero.
bootstrap — resampling your own test cases · with replacement — a block may be drawn twice, or not at all
Efron 1979 · Künsch 1989 (blocks) · code: A10 · your 7 Dec table: paired 95% block intervals, ≥ 1,000 resamples, the same draws for every row
2 min. This is the slide Week 10 promised: you report block-bootstrap intervals. Why you need it: your test set is one sample of the cases you could have had. Had you drawn other basins, other tiles, other weeks, every score in your table would be a little different — and so would the difference between two rows. The bootstrap estimates how much, using only the test set you have. Follow the picture. One: write down the error of each model on each test case, in the same order — two aligned strips — and cut the test set into blocks that are independent of each other. Two: draw whole blocks with replacement, as many as you have; here blocks 3, 1, 3, 4. Use that one draw for both rows. Three: compute your score on the resampled cases for each row and subtract: one number. Four: do it a thousand times or more. The numbers pile up into a histogram; cut off 2.5% at each end; what is left is the 95% interval of the difference. If zero is outside it, the rows differ. If zero is inside, you may not write "better". Now the two words. Block: the bootstrap assumes the things you resample are independent. Two forecasts twelve hours apart are not; two tiles from the same scene are not; two days in the same flood are not. Resample the unit that is independent — the ten-day window, the basin, the scene, the event — and everything inside it comes along. If you resample single samples you are pretending to have more independent evidence than you do, and the interval comes out too narrow. Paired: use the same draw for every row. A hard case is hard for every model; that shared difficulty is most of the variation in each score, and it cancels in the difference only if both rows got the same cases. The next slide shows both effects in numbers. The function is nine lines; it is on A10 and it has been tested against today's figure.

Appendix A7 · The latitude weight

A7 · The weight does not make the model better — it decides where the model is good

  • Model C, no weight: global Z500 error within 2% of A’s — 3 to 9% higher in the tropics, 12% lower in the polar caps.
  • On a latitude–longitude grid a cell at 60° covers half the area of one at the equator. Unweighted, the many small polar cells count as much as the large ones, and the network spends its capacity there.
latitude weight — cos(latitude) divided by its mean over the rows: each cell counts in the loss, and in the score, by the area it covers
Models A and C, 1,440 forecasts · polar caps: at day 1 · 2 m temperature: 4 to 20% higher in the tropics · the interval on C − A: slide 18
Reference only; it answers the first discussion question of the reading. cos 60° = 0.5, so an unweighted loss counts a square kilometre at 60° twice as much as one at the equator, and at 87° twenty times. The global score barely moves, because the weighted score hardly sees the poles; the trade shows only when the error is split by latitude — which is why Talk 5 asks for the score split by region.

Appendix A9 · Scores

A9 · Today’s scores in NumPy: weights, RMSE, ACC, fair CRPS, spread–skill

def lat_weights(lat):                        # lat: degrees, one value per grid row
    w = np.cos(np.deg2rad(lat))
    return w / w.mean()                      # mean 1, so a weighted mean is an area mean

def rmse(f, o, w):                           # f, o: (forecasts, lat, lon); w: (lat,)
    return np.sqrt(np.mean(w[None, :, None] * (f - o) ** 2))

def acc(f, o, c, w):                         # c: the climatology at each forecast's valid time
    fa, oa = f - c, o - c                    # anomalies
    m = lambda x: np.mean(w[None, :, None] * x, axis=(1, 2))
    return np.mean(m(fa * oa) / np.sqrt(m(fa ** 2) * m(oa ** 2)))     # one value per forecast, then their mean
def crps_fair(X, o, w):                      # X: (members, forecasts, lat, lon), two members or more
    M = X.shape[0]
    err = np.abs(X - o).mean(0)              # the members' mean absolute error ...
    gap = sum(np.abs(X[i] - X[j]) for i in range(M) for j in range(M)) / (2 * M * (M - 1))
    return np.mean(w[None, :, None] * (err - gap))                    # ... minus half their mean distance apart

def spread_skill(X, o, w):                   # 1 for a reliable ensemble, below 1 when it is too narrow
    M = X.shape[0]
    spread = np.sqrt(np.mean(w[None, :, None] * X.var(0, ddof=1)))
    return np.sqrt((M + 1) / M) * spread / rmse(X.mean(0), o, w)
  • o is the reference (here ERA5). A single forecast’s CRPS is its mean absolute error, so one scale serves both.
Tested: persistence at day 1 gives RMSE 570.31 and ACC 0.7385, as on slide 9 · a reliable toy ensemble gives spread–skill 1.00 · definitions: Rasp et al. 2024
Reference only. The same weights enter every score. The anomaly correlation is computed per forecast and then averaged; the fair CRPS divides the member-to-member term by M(M − 1), not M squared, so a small ensemble is not penalised for its size.

Appendix A10 · The interval

A10 · The paired block bootstrap in nine lines, and how to choose the block

def paired_block_ci(err_a, err_b, block_id, stat, n_boot=2000, seed=0):
    blocks = [np.flatnonzero(block_id == b) for b in np.unique(block_id)]
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    d = np.empty(n_boot)
    for r in range(n_boot):
        draw = rng.integers(0, len(blocks), len(blocks))       # whole blocks, with replacement
        i = np.concatenate([blocks[k] for k in draw])          # the SAME cases for both rows
        d[r] = stat(err_b[i]) - stat(err_a[i])
    return np.percentile(d, [2.5, 97.5])                       # the 95% interval of (row B - row A)

score = lambda se: np.sqrt(se.mean())                 # se: squared error per forecast, area-weighted -> RMSE
