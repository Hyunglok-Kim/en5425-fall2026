Part 1 · 10 min

Backprop as vector–Jacobian products

Timing: minutes 0–10. Promise up front: no derivation theater — the full chain-rule → VJP derivation is in the handout. Here we take the operational view: what backprop computes, what it costs, and where the memory goes — because that memory bill is why gradient checkpointing exists in Week 12.

Part 1 · 10 min

Backprop never builds a Jacobian

  • Layer f: ℝⁿ → ℝᵐ has Jacobian J ∈ ℝᵐˣⁿ. For one transformer layer, m·n ≈ 10⁸ — you never materialize it.
  • Reverse mode propagates a vector–Jacobian product: the row vector ḡᵀ·J, layer by layer, from scalar loss back to parameters.
  • One backward pass costs ≈ 2× the forward FLOPs — independent of parameter count. That is why 10⁹-parameter gradients are affordable.
  • Autograd = forward pass records the graph + operand tensors; backward replays it in reverse, one VJP per node.
  • Full derivation (chain rule → VJP recursion, with shapes) in this week's handout.
Handout: backprop derivation · Griewank & Walther, Evaluating Derivatives (2008)
~3 min. The one sentence to land: "gradient of a scalar w.r.t. everything costs about two forward passes." Ask them: what does the forward pass have to keep around to make the backward replay possible? That question is the next slide.

Part 1 · 10 min

The forward pass buys the backward pass by saving activations

Each VJP needs the tensors the node saw going forward — they must sit in memory until the backward pass reaches them.
~3 min. Walk the diagram left→right (forward, dashed boxes = tensors autograd stashes), then right→left (each accent arrow is one VJP, consuming a stashed tensor). Punchline: nothing dashed can be freed until backward consumes it. That's the memory story on the next slide.

Part 1 · 10 min

Activations, not weights, dominate training memory

  • Every saved tensor is a debt the backward pass collects: activation memory ∝ depth × batch × tokens × width.
  • ViT-L, batch 256: weights + Adam states ≈ 3.6 GB — activations ≈ tens of GB. Activations set your batch-size ceiling, not parameters.
  • Gradient checkpointing: keep ~O(√L) of the activations, recompute the rest during backward — ≈30% extra FLOPs for 5–10× less activation memory.
  • When you OOM fine-tuning an FM in Week 12, the fix is checkpointing / batch / precision — not a smaller model. Remember this slide.
Chen et al. 2016, arXiv:1604.06174 (checkpointing) · callback target: W12 fine-tuning lab
~3 min, then transition. Quick mental arithmetic on the ViT-L numbers: 0.3B params × 4 bytes ≈ 1.2 GB, Adam doubles it with m and v. Then point at batch×tokens×width×depth and let them see it dwarfs 3.6 GB. Transition: "so we can compute gradients cheaply — what do we do with them? The recipe. Minute 10, Part 2."

Part 2 · 15 min

The optimizer recipe

AdamW · warmup + cosine · decoupled decay · clipping — one recipe, every FM.

Timing: minutes 10–25. Framing: this is not a menu, it is THE recipe — by the end of this part they should be able to read the optimization paragraph of any FM paper in 30 seconds. Five content slides, ~2.5 min each.

Part 2 · 15 min

AdamW: normalize by gradient history, decay the weights separately

mt = β₁·mt−1 + (1−β₁)·gt   vt = β₂·vt−1 + (1−β₂)·gt²
m̂ = mt / (1−β₁ᵗ)  v̂ = vt / (1−β₂ᵗ)
θt = θt−1 − ηt · ( m̂ / (√v̂ + ε) + λ·θt−1 )

  • The ratio m̂/√v̂ makes the step size ≈ ηt per parameter, whatever the raw gradient scale — a signal-to-noise step.
  • Bias correction (1−βᵗ) only matters for the first ~1/(1−β) steps — exactly when training is most fragile.
  • Typical FM settings: β₁ = 0.9, β₂ = 0.95, ε = 10⁻⁸, λ = 0.05–0.1.
Kingma & Ba 2015 (Adam) · Loshchilov & Hutter 2019, arXiv:1711.05101 (AdamW)
~3 min. Read the update aloud once, slowly: momentum in the numerator, running second moment in the denominator, and — separately, outside the adaptive ratio — the decay term. That "outside" is the entire AdamW point; flag it and promise the distinction in two slides. Note β₂ = 0.95 (not 0.999) is the large-model convention: shorter variance memory tolerates noisy spikes better.

Part 2 · 15 min

Warmup protects the noisy start; cosine anneals the finish

  • Warmup: η ramps 0 → ηmax while v̂ statistics are still garbage — skip it and step 50 diverges.
  • Cosine: ηt = ηmin + ½(ηmax − ηmin)(1 + cos(π·t/T)) — long quiet tail, no step-schedule cliffs; T = total steps, fixed in advance.
~3 min. Intuition for warmup: Adam divides by √v̂; with 10 samples of variance, that denominator is noise, so early steps can be huge in random directions. Intuition for cosine: most of the decay budget is spent slowly in the middle, and the tail lets the model settle into a basin. Note T is a commitment — cosine requires deciding the compute budget up front, which is very FM-culture.

Part 2 · 15 min

In Adam, an L2 penalty is not weight decay

L2 penalty (Adam + λ‖θ‖²)

  • λθ is added to gt → enters m and v → gets divided by √v̂.
  • Parameters with large gradient history are barely decayed at all.
  • Regularization strength entangled with the adaptive state — λ and η can't be tuned independently.

Decoupled decay (AdamW)

  • −ηt·λ·θ applied directly, outside the adaptive ratio.
  • Uniform multiplicative shrinkage of every weight, every step.
  • Empirically recovers SGD-like generalization; this is why "AdamW" is in every FM paper and plain Adam is not.
Loshchilov & Hutter 2019, arXiv:1711.05101
~2.5 min. This is the classic subtle bug: PyTorch Adam(weight_decay=...) implements the LEFT column; AdamW implements the right. Same argument name, different mathematics. Quick check question: which parameters does L2-in-Adam fail to regularize? (The ones with the largest v̂ — often exactly the ones overfitting.)

Part 2 · 15 min

Gradient clipping is loss-spike insurance, not a tuning knob

  • Global-norm clipping: g ← g · min(1, c/‖g‖₂) — direction preserved, magnitude capped.
  • Rare batches (an unusual storm, a corrupted chip, an fp16 edge) give ‖g‖ 10–100× typical; one unclipped step can undo hours of training.
  • GraphCast clips at c = 32; most LLM recipes use c = 1.0 — the presence of clipping matters far more than the value.
  • Monitor the clipped fraction in W&B: if >5% of steps clip, your LR is too high or your data has a problem.
Lam et al. 2023, Science 382:1416 (GraphCast supplement)
~2 min. Emphasize the diagnostic use: clipping silently hides pathologies, so log the clip rate. This is the first of many "what to watch in W&B" habits — W&B becomes mandatory today, in the lab.

Part 2 · 15 min

Regularization, compressed to one pass

  • Early stopping on a validation metric is the one regularizer you always use — patience in epochs, restore the best checkpoint, never the last.
  • Augmentation encodes invariances — but physical fields have preferred directions: never N–S flip a wind field; Coriolis is not flip-invariant.
  • Dropout / weight decay trade variance for bias; at pretraining scale, decay does most of the work and dropout often goes to 0.
  • Double descent, in one line: past the interpolation threshold, bigger models can generalize better — part of why FMs are big at all.
Belkin et al. 2019, PNAS 116:15849 (double descent)
~2.5 min. The augmentation bullet is the geoscience twist — invariances are physical claims, not free lunch. Early stopping foreshadows Part 3: "early stopping is only as honest as the validation set it watches — hold that thought."

Part 3 · 25 min

Leakage-safe evaluation

The geoscience failure mode: your test set is not new data — it's your training set, shifted 5 km or 1 day.

Timing: minutes 25–50 — the headline block; protect it. Claim to establish: in environmental data, random splits do not measure generalization, and the error is not small or subtle — it flips paper conclusions. Everything this course grades — memos, labs, projects — will be held to the standard set in the next eight slides.

Part 3 · 25 min

Environmental samples are not i.i.d. — nearby pixels share their cause

  • Tobler's law, quantified: soil moisture, biomass, temperature have spatial correlation ranges of 10–1000 km, set by rainfall, terrain, and climate zones.
  • Every ML split assumes test points are independent draws. A test pixel 5 km from a training pixel is, statistically, the same draw.
  • The variogram range — the distance where correlation dies — is the scale your split must respect.
  • The model is not cheating; the evaluation is: memorized geography scores as skill.
Tobler 1970 · Roberts et al. 2017, Ecography 40:913
~3 min. Anchor with a concrete pair: two ISMN stations 8 km apart in Iowa see the same rain, same soil, same season — knowing one IS knowing the other. Then the i.i.d. sentence: every off-the-shelf train_test_split call encodes an independence assumption the atmosphere refuses to satisfy.

Part 3 · 25 min

What a random split does to a map

~3 min on this figure alone — it is the core picture of the week. Left: every accent (test) dot has a training neighbor within the correlation range; the dashed 5-km pair is the leak. Right: the shaded block is the fold — every test point is at least a block-width from any training point. Same data, same model, two different questions being asked.

Part 3 · 25 min

Time leaks the same way: today is mostly yesterday

  • Daily surface soil moisture: lag-1 autocorrelation ≈ 0.9–0.95; drydowns persist for days to weeks. Streamflow and LST behave the same way.
  • Random split over days: a test day sandwiched between two training days is scored on interpolating memory, not modeling physics.
  • Seasonality is a second leak channel — the same phase of the annual cycle recurs in training even when the exact day is held out.
  • Consequence: temporal splits must be contiguous blocks (whole years), with a gap at the seam.
~2.5 min. Concrete test: "predict soil moisture for July 15 given July 14 and July 16 in training" — persistence alone scores brilliantly, no model needed. Whenever a paper reports random-in-time CV on daily data, its skill ceiling is set by autocorrelation, not by the model.

Part 3 · 25 min

Random splits lie — and the lie is large

  • Ploton et al. 2020, tropical forest biomass from satellite features: R² ≈ 0.53 under random CV → R² ≈ 0.14 under spatial CV. Same model, same data.
  • The pattern replicates across geo-ML: species distribution, air quality, hydrology (basin models collapse on ungauged basins).
  • Optimism gap ≔ score(random split) − score(blocked split). Report it; don't hide it.
  • A high random-split score is a claim about interpolation near your stations — nothing more. Today's lab measures this gap on CAMELS-US.
Ploton et al. 2020, Nat. Commun. 11:4540 · Meyer & Pebesma 2022, Nat. Commun. 13:2208
~3 min. The Ploton numbers are the slide — 0.53 to 0.14 is not a correction, it's a different conclusion; the mapped product looked publishable and was largely geography memorization. Define the optimism gap on the board and tell them it is a required row in today's lab table and in every project report this semester.

Part 3 · 25 min

Autocorrelation shrinks your effective sample size

AR(1) with autocorrelation ρ: neff = n · (1−ρ) / (1+ρ)  ρ = 0.9 → n/19 · ρ = 0.95 → n/39

  • Ten years of daily observations at one station: n = 3650, ρ ≈ 0.95 → ≈ 94 independent samples.
  • Error bars computed with n are far too tight — "significant" model improvements evaporate under blocked resampling.
  • Blocked CV is the honest approximation: folds separated at the correlation scale behave like independent draws of size ≈ neff.
Wilks 2011, Statistical Methods in the Atmospheric Sciences, §5 (serial correlation)
~3 min. Do the arithmetic live: 3650/39 ≈ 94 — a decade of daily data carries about three months of information. Then connect back: leakage inflates the score, autocorrelation inflates the confidence — random splits get BOTH wrong at once.

Part 3 · 25 min

Spatial-block design: block at or beyond the correlation range

  • Estimate the range from a variogram of the target (or model residuals); block size ≥ range.
  • Assign whole blocks to folds; add a buffer strip around test blocks if the range is uncertain.
  • Match block geometry to the deployment question: random blocks → "new nearby areas"; leave-region-out → "new climates" (extrapolation).
  • Too-small blocks re-admit leakage; too-large blocks starve training. There is no free choice — report the block size with the result.
Roberts et al. 2017, Ecography 40:913 · Meyer et al. 2019 (spatial variable selection)
~2.5 min. The third bullet is the intellectual center: the split IS the scientific question. "How well does this model do?" is ill-posed — do you mean 10 km away or on a new continent? Choose the split that matches the claim you intend to publish.

Part 3 · 25 min

Temporal holdout: whole years, and mind the seams

  • Hold out contiguous years — never shuffled days, never shuffled months.
  • Leave a gap at the seam ≥ the system's memory: days for weather, weeks for soil moisture, months for groundwater.
  • Nonstationarity is a feature of the test: held-out years should contain unseen extremes (e.g., a drought year the model never trained on).
  • Normalization statistics from training years only — computing the climatology over all years is the classic silent leak.
~2.5 min. The fourth bullet catches real papers: standardizing with the full-period mean quietly hands the model the test years' climate. In today's lab and every lab after, normalization stats are computed inside the training fold — the agent will scaffold it; you must verify it.

Part 3 · 25 min

WeatherBench2: year separation as the professional standard

  • Common ERA5 ground truth, fixed metrics (RMSE, ACC on Z500/T850/…), strict train/val/test year separation — every model on the leaderboard obeys it.
  • GraphCast, Pangu, NeuralGCM, and the IFS baseline are all scored on the same held-out year: that is why the comparison is believable.
Rasp et al. 2024, arXiv:2308.15560 · weatherbench2.readthedocs.io
~3 min. This is the aspirational example: an entire subfield agreed on one leakage-safe protocol, and suddenly cross-paper comparison works. Contrast with fields where every paper invents its own split. The dashed line is the discipline: no tuning decision — not even "which checkpoint to submit" — may look at 2020.

Part 3 · 25 min

The leakage checklist you will run on every project

  • 1 · Does the split match the deployment claim (interpolation vs new region vs new year)?
  • 2 · Are spatial blocks ≥ the autocorrelation range, with whole blocks per fold?
  • 3 · Are temporal folds contiguous, with a gap ≥ system memory at the seam?
  • 4 · Are all preprocessing statistics (normalization, climatology, feature selection) computed on training folds only?
  • 5 · Is the optimism gap (random vs blocked) measured and reported?
This checklist is the rubric for the lab post-mortem and for project evaluations in W14–16.
Minute ~48. Read it once, then say: this is not advice, it is the grading rubric — memos, labs, and final projects are checked against these five lines. Transition: "let's run the checklist forward on a real design problem — global soil moisture, ten minutes."

Part 4 · 10 min

Worked example: a reviewer-proof split for global soil moisture

Timing: minutes 50–60. Format: pose the design problem, expose the three leak paths, then build the three-way holdout. This is also literally DQ2 in tonight's reading memo — tell them so at the end of the part.

Part 4 · 10 min

The setup — and three ways the model can cheat

  • Task: daily surface soil moisture from precipitation, LST, NDVI, soil texture, terrain. Labels: ~2,800 ISMN stations, heavily clustered in CONUS and Europe.
  • Leak 1 · space: many station pairs sit <10 km apart — well inside the correlation range.
  • Leak 2 · time: lag-1 autocorrelation ≈ 0.95 — any shuffled-day split is persistence in disguise.
  • Leak 3 · instrument: each network (SCAN, USCRN, SNOTEL, …) has its own sensors and calibration — network-specific bias is learnable and looks like skill.
  • A random split leaves all three open. A competent reviewer rejects in one paragraph.
ISMN: Dorigo et al. 2021, HESS 25:5749
~3 min. Leak 3 is the one students miss: hold out stations but not networks, and the model still recognizes "this reads like a SCAN sensor" and exploits the network's calibration offset. Instrument identity is a feature you did not mean to provide.

Part 4 · 10 min

Hold out along every axis the model could exploit

By region

  • Leave whole continents / Köppen zones out.
  • Tests spatial transfer: "does it work where we have no stations?"
  • Kills Leak 1.

By year

  • Train ≤ 2018, test 2020–21, gap at the seam.
  • Tests temporal transfer under unseen climate anomalies.
  • Kills Leak 2.

By network

  • Fit on SCAN + others, test on USCRN.
  • Tests instrument transfer; bounds calibration-bias learning.
  • Kills Leak 3.
~3 min. One axis per leak — the symmetry is the takeaway. Ask which single axis they'd keep if forced to choose one, and why the answer depends on the deployment claim (a global map product needs region-holdout most; an operational forecast needs year-holdout most).

Part 4 · 10 min

The reviewer-proof protocol is the intersection, not the union

  • Primary score: held-out regions × held-out years simultaneously — unseen place in an unseen year.
  • Secondary: network-holdout to bound the instrument-bias contribution.
  • All tuning (sweeps, early stopping, checkpoint choice) inside training folds; the test partition is touched once.
  • Report a per-region table, not one global mean — the model's failures in data-sparse regions are the scientific result.
  • This is DQ2 in tonight's memo. Use today's vocabulary: range, block, gap, optimism gap.
~3 min, end at minute 60. Stress "touched once": the test set is spent the moment it influences any decision. And the per-region table point previews the FM-evaluation weeks — global means hide exactly the regions environmental science cares about. Transition to the reading discussion.
