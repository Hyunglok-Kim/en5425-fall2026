Part 1 · 10 min

Backpropagation, derived

Three steps of the chain rule, then the general rule — and the memory bill it creates.

Timing: minutes 0–10. The derivation is four slides; at lecture pace that is ~5 minutes — read the equations aloud slowly and point at each symbol. The remaining slides give the cost and the memory story, which is why gradient checkpointing exists in Week 12.

Part 1 · Derivation · step 0

The network we will differentiate

input x ∈ ℝd₀
layer 1 z₁ = W₁x + b₁, W₁ ∈ ℝd₁×d₀ → h = σ(z₁) (σ applied to each entry)
layer 2 z₂ = W₂h + b₂, W₂ ∈ ℝd₂×d₁
loss L = ½‖z₂ − y‖² = ½ Σi (z₂i − yi)² (one scalar)

  • Goal: ∂L/∂W₂, ∂L/∂b₂, ∂L/∂W₁, ∂L/∂b₁ — the gradient of one scalar with respect to every parameter.
  • Everything that follows is the chain rule applied three times, keeping careful track of shapes.
Read: Goodfellow §6.5, pp. 200–220 · Dive into Deep Learning §5.3 · CS231n “Backpropagation, Intuitions” + Johnson’s “Derivatives” handout — linked on the Week 2 page
~1 min. Fix the notation and shapes on this slide and the rest is mechanical. Squared-error loss keeps the algebra clean; swapping in cross-entropy changes only step 1. Emphasize: L is ONE number — that fact is what makes the whole reverse pass cheap.

Part 1 · Derivation · step 1 of 3

Output layer: one entry at a time, then in matrix form

∂L/∂z₂i = z₂i − yi ⟹ g₂ ≔ ∂L/∂z₂ = z₂ − y ∈ ℝd₂
z₂i = Σj W₂[i,j]·hj + b₂i ⟹ ∂z₂i/∂W₂[i,j] = hj (and no other row of W₂ touches z₂i)
chain rule: ∂L/∂W₂[i,j] = g₂i · hj ⟹ ∂L/∂W₂ = g₂ hᵀ (outer product, d₂×d₁) · ∂L/∂b₂ = g₂

  • Each weight W₂[i,j] influences the loss through exactly one number, z₂i — so its gradient is one product: (how much L cares about z₂i) × (how much z₂i moves per unit of W₂[i,j]).
  • Note what the formula needs: g₂, and h — the layer’s input, saved from the forward pass. Remember this.
~1.5 min. Do the middle line slowly: differentiate the sum, everything with j′ ≠ j dies, h_j survives. The outer-product form is just all (i,j) pairs at once. The bolded observation — "gradient with respect to weights = (incoming gradient) times (stored input)" — is the punchline the memory slide will collect on.

Part 1 · Derivation · step 2 of 3

Backward through the hidden layer — sum over every path

hj feeds every z₂i ⟹ ∂L/∂hj = Σi g₂i·W₂[i,j] ⟹ ∂L/∂h = W₂ᵀ g₂ ∈ ℝd₁
hj = σ(z₁j) ⟹ ∂L/∂z₁j = σ′(z₁j) · (W₂ᵀg₂)j ⟹ g₁ ≔ σ′(z₁) ⊙ W₂ᵀg₂ (⊙ = entrywise)
same pattern as step 1: ∂L/∂W₁ = g₁ xᵀ · ∂L/∂b₁ = g₁

  • The Σi is the multivariable chain rule: hj reaches the loss through d₂ separate paths, and their contributions add.
  • The recursion is now visible — every layer repeats two moves: pass back Wᵀg · turn gradient into weights with (g)(stored input)ᵀ.
~1.5 min. The Σ-over-paths line is where students who memorized backprop actually learn it — one hidden unit, many downstream consumers, contributions add. Then point at the symmetry with step 1: the algorithm is two alternating moves, all the way down, however deep the network is.

Part 1 · Derivation · step 3 of 3

The general rule: vector–Jacobian products, right to left

for any map f: ℝⁿ → ℝᵐ, the Jacobian is J[i,j] = ∂fi/∂xj ∈ ℝm×n
chain over the whole network: ∂L/∂x = J₁ᵀ J₂ᵀ ⋯ Jkᵀ · ∇outL
computed right to left, each step is ḡ ← Jᵀḡ — a vector–Jacobian product, never matrix × matrix

  • Right to left works because L is a scalar: the running quantity ḡ stays a vector the whole way down.
  • The Jacobian of z = Wh with respect to W has shape m×(m·n) — for one transformer layer that is ~10⁸ entries. You never build it: the product Jᵀḡ collapses to the outer product ḡhᵀ from step 1.
  • Automatic differentiation = the forward pass records the graph and saves each operand; the backward pass replays it in reverse, one vector–Jacobian product per node. That is the whole of PyTorch’s loss.backward().
Read: Goodfellow §6.5.2 (chain rule) · Carnegie Mellon “Deep Learning Systems”, lecture 4: Automatic Differentiation · Griewank & Walther, Evaluating Derivatives (2008)
~1.5 min. The order-of-multiplication point deserves the emphasis: left-to-right would be Jacobian-times-Jacobian (huge matrices); right-to-left keeps everything vector-sized because the thing we differentiate is one scalar. Forward-mode exists too and wins when outputs outnumber inputs — never our case in training.

Part 1 · Cost

Counting the operations: backward ≈ two forwards

forward through one linear layer W h (W ∈ ℝm×n): ≈ 2mn floating-point operations
backward, same layer: ∂L/∂h = Wᵀg (≈ 2mn) + ∂L/∂W = g hᵀ (≈ 2mn over a batch)
⟹ backward ≈ 2 × forward, whole training step ≈ 3 × forward — at any parameter count

  • This is why gradients of a 10⁹-parameter model are affordable: the price of knowing ∂L/∂(everything) is two extra forward passes, not 10⁹ extra evaluations.
  • Contrast with finite differences: one loss evaluation per parameter — 10⁹ forward passes for the same information.
Same count in Roger Grosse’s University of Toronto lecture notes on backpropagation: one multiply–add per weight forward, two backward.
~1 min. The 3× rule is worth committing to memory — it is how you estimate training cost from inference cost on any model, and it reappears in every scaling-law discussion later in the course (the 6·N·D approximation for transformer training FLOPs is this same bookkeeping).

Part 1 · Memory

The forward pass buys the backward pass by saving activations

Look back at steps 1–2: ∂L/∂W₂ needed h, ∂L/∂W₁ needed x, σ′ needed z₁ — every dashed box is one of those saved tensors.
~2 min. Walk the diagram left→right (forward, dashed boxes = tensors autograd stashes), then right→left (each accent arrow consumes a stashed tensor — and now the class has personally derived WHY each one is needed). Punchline: nothing dashed can be freed until backward consumes it.

Part 1 · Memory

Activations, not weights, dominate training memory

  • Every saved tensor is a debt the backward pass collects: activation memory ∝ depth × batch size × sequence length × width.
  • A large Vision Transformer (ViT-Large, 0.3 billion parameters), batch 256: weights + Adam’s two moment tensors ≈ 3.6 GB — activations ≈ tens of gigabytes. Activations set your batch-size ceiling, not parameters.
  • Gradient checkpointing: keep only ~√(number of layers) of the activations, recompute the rest segment-by-segment during backward — ≈ one extra forward pass (+33% step cost) for 5–10× less activation memory.
  • When you run out of graphics-card memory fine-tuning a foundation model in Week 12, the fix is checkpointing / batch size / numeric precision — not a smaller model. Remember this slide.
Chen et al. 2016, arXiv:1604.06174 (checkpointing) · callback target: Week 12 fine-tuning lab
~2 min, then the video slide. Quick mental arithmetic on the ViT-Large numbers: 0.3 billion × 4 bytes ≈ 1.2 GB; Adam keeps m and v, tripling it. Then point at batch × tokens × width × depth and let them see it dwarfs 3.6 GB. The √L trick: split the net into √L segments, keep only segment boundaries, recompute inside one segment at a time — memory √L, extra compute one forward.

The optimizer recipe, derived

AdamW · warmup + cosine · decoupled decay · clipping — one recipe, every foundation model.

Timing: minutes 10–25. Framing: this is not a menu, it is THE recipe — by the end of this part they should be able to read the optimization paragraph of any foundation-model paper in thirty seconds, and derive each ingredient if pressed. Eight content slides, ~2 min each.

Part 2 · Derivation

Gradient descent falls out of one line of calculus

first-order Taylor: L(θ + Δ) ≈ L(θ) + ∇L(θ)ᵀΔ
choose Δ = −η·∇L(θ): L(θ − η∇L) ≈ L(θ) − η‖∇L‖² < L(θ) for small enough η
⟹ θ ← θ − η ∇L — the steepest step that first-order information can justify

  • Everything in this part is a repair to this rule for the realities of deep networks: noisy gradients, curvature that varies by 10⁶ across parameters, and budgets fixed in advance.
  • Minibatches: the batch gradient ĝ = (1/B)Σ∇ℓi over B random samples has E[ĝ] = ∇L (unbiased) and variance ∝ 1/B — noise falls like √B while cost grows like B, so moderate batches + many steps win.
Read: Goodfellow §8.3, pp. 290–296 · Dive into Deep Learning §12.3–12.5 (gradient descent → stochastic → minibatch)
~2 min. The Taylor line is the entire theory a practitioner needs: gradient descent is not mystical, it is "the linear approximation says downhill is this way, take a small step." The unbiasedness of the minibatch gradient is one line — expectation of a mean of random samples — say it, don't write it.

Part 2 · Derivation

Momentum is an exponential moving average — unroll it once

definition: mt = β·mt−1 + (1−β)·gt, m₀ = 0
unroll: mt = (1−β)·( gt + β·gt−1 + β²·gt−2 + ⋯ + βt−1·g₁ )
⟹ a weighted average of past gradients, weights decaying by β per step — effective window ≈ 1/(1−β)

  • β = 0.9 → averages roughly the last 10 gradients; β = 0.95 → last 20; β = 0.999 → last 1000.
  • Averaging cancels minibatch noise in directions that flip sign and accumulates speed in directions that persist — a low-pass filter on the gradient signal.
  • Keep the unrolled line in view — the bias-correction derivation two slides ahead reads directly off it.
Read: Goodfellow §8.3.2 (momentum, from p. 292) · Dive into Deep Learning §12.6 (momentum as leaky averages)
~2 min. Do the unroll live in one substitution: m_t = (1−β)g_t + β·m_{t−1}, expand m_{t−1} once, spot the geometric pattern, wave the rest. The 1/(1−β) window: weights drop by factor e after about 1/(1−β) steps — the practical translation students should carry.

Part 2 · Derivation

Adam: the same trick twice — momentum for the mean, and for the scale

first moment: mt = β₁·mt−1 + (1−β₁)·gt (the direction, denoised)
second moment: vt = β₂·vt−1 + (1−β₂)·gt² (the typical squared size, per parameter)
update: θt = θt−1 − ηt · m̂t / (√v̂t + ε) (m̂, v̂ = bias-corrected, next slide)

  • Thought experiment — constant gradient g: then m̂ → g, v̂ → g², and the step is η·g/|g| = η·sign(g). The raw gradient scale cancels entirely.
  • So every parameter moves ≈ ηt per step regardless of whether its gradients are 10⁻⁶ or 10³ — one learning rate serves layers with wildly different curvature. That is the whole reason Adam took over.
  • Foundation-model settings: β₁ = 0.9, β₂ = 0.95, ε = 10⁻⁸ — note β₂ = 0.95, not the old default 0.999: a ~20-step variance memory reacts faster to loss spikes at scale.
Kingma & Ba 2015 (Adam) · Read: Goodfellow §8.5.3, pp. 305–306 · Dive into Deep Learning §12.10
~2 min. The constant-gradient thought experiment is the fastest honest way to see what the ratio does — run it aloud. Real gradients are noisy, so the step becomes roughly η × (signal / total magnitude) ≤ η: big confident steps where the gradient is consistent, small ones where it thrashes.

Part 2 · Derivation

Bias correction: why m̂ divides by (1 − β₁ᵗ), exactly

take expectations of the unrolled average, assuming E[gk] = μ:
E[mt] = (1−β)·(1 + β + β² + ⋯ + βt−1)·μ = (1−β)·(1−βᵗ)/(1−β)·μ = (1−βᵗ)·μ
⟹ m̂t ≔ mt/(1−βᵗ) has E[m̂t] = μ (same argument gives v̂t = vt/(1−β₂ᵗ))

  • The accent step is the finite geometric series Σk=0t−1 βᵏ = (1−βᵗ)/(1−β) — the only identity in the whole derivation.
  • Sanity check at t = 1: m₁ = (1−β)g₁ and 1−β¹ = 1−β, so m̂₁ = g₁ exactly — the correction undoes the zero initialization completely on step one.
  • It matters precisely when training is most fragile: for t ≪ 1/(1−β₂), uncorrected v is far too small → the step η·m/√v would be far too big. After ~1/(1−β) steps, βᵗ → 0 and the correction fades away.
The same three lines, with runnable code: Dive into Deep Learning §12.10.1
~2 min. This is the derivation students pretend to know — it is three lines, do all three. Emphasize what it is NOT: not a heuristic warm-start, but an exact unbiasing of an exponentially-weighted average that was initialized at zero. It still doesn't fix the early VARIANCE of v̂ — which is why warmup exists anyway, next slide.

Part 2 · Schedule

Warmup protects the noisy start; cosine anneals the finish

ηt = ηmin + ½·(ηmax − ηmin)·(1 + cos(π·t/T)) check: t = 0 → cos 0 = 1 → ηmax · t = T → cos π = −1 → ηmin; slope ≈ 0 at both ends

  • Warmup: bias correction fixed the mean of v̂, not its variance — with ~10 samples, √v̂ is still noise, and dividing by noise makes huge steps in random directions. Ramping η from 0 keeps those early steps harmless. Skip it and step 50 diverges.
  • Cosine: gentle leaving of ηmax, long quiet tail for settling into a basin — and T is fixed in advance: the schedule is a commitment to a compute budget, very foundation-model culture.
Read: Dive into Deep Learning §12.11 (cosine scheduler §12.11.3.3, warmup §12.11.3.4 — the one course resource that covers both)
~2 min. Connect backward explicitly: "the previous slide proved the correction fixes E[v]; it cannot fix Var[v] with ten samples — that residual noise is what warmup absorbs." Endpoint check of the cosine formula takes ten seconds and inoculates against sign errors when they implement it in the lab.

Part 2 · Derivation

An ℓ₂ penalty and weight decay: identical in plain gradient descent…

penalized loss: L̃(θ) = L(θ) + (λ/2)·‖θ‖² ⟹ ∇L̃ = g + λθ
gradient-descent step: θ ← θ − η(g + λθ) = (1 − ηλ)·θ − ηg
⟹ “add λθ to the gradient” and “shrink weights by (1−ηλ) each step” are the same rule

…but in Adam the λθ term rides through the adaptive ratio: step ∝ m̂(g + λθ) / √v̂(g + λθ)
a parameter with a large gradient history (large v̂) has its decay term divided by √v̂ → barely decayed —
often exactly the weights that are overfitting. The equivalence is broken.

~2 min. Two boards: derive the SGD equivalence completely (it is two lines), then show where Adam breaks it — the decay term gets normalized along with everything else, so regularization strength silently varies per parameter and entangles with η. Cliffhanger: "the fix is embarrassingly simple," next slide.

Part 2 · Derivation

…so AdamW moves the decay outside the ratio

AdamW: θt = θt−1 − ηt·m̂t/(√v̂t + ε) − ηt·λ·θt−1
the adaptive ratio sees only the data gradient; the decay is a uniform multiplicative shrink (1 − ηtλ) again

  • Restores the plain-gradient-descent behaviour the derivation promised: every weight decays at the same rate, and λ and η can be tuned independently.
  • Empirically recovers the generalization gap between Adam and well-tuned stochastic gradient descent — this is why every foundation-model paper writes “AdamW” and none write “Adam + ℓ₂”.
  • The classic silent bug: PyTorch Adam(weight_decay=…) implements the broken left-hand version; AdamW(weight_decay=…) implements this slide. Same argument name, different mathematics.
  • Typical foundation-model value: λ = 0.05–0.1 (with η ~ 10⁻³–10⁻⁴).
Loshchilov & Hutter 2019, arXiv:1711.05101 · Read: Goodfellow §7.1, pp. 226–230 · Dive into Deep Learning §3.7 (the (1−ηλ) shrinkage derivation)
~1.5 min. Check question for the room: which parameters does ℓ₂-inside-Adam fail to regularize? (Largest v̂ — the busiest weights.) Then the PyTorch trap — worth a full sentence because half of them will hit it this semester in their own sweeps.

Part 2 · Recipe

Gradient clipping is loss-spike insurance, not a tuning knob

g ← g · min(1, c/‖g‖₂) if ‖g‖₂ ≤ c: untouched · else: same direction, norm exactly c

  • Rare batches (an unusual storm in the data, a corrupted file, a 16-bit floating-point edge case) give ‖g‖ 10–100× typical; one unclipped step can undo hours of training.
  • GraphCast clips at c = 32; most large-language-model recipes use c = 1.0 — the presence of clipping matters far more than the value.
  • Monitor the clipped fraction in Weights & Biases: if more than ~5% of steps clip, your learning rate is too high or your data has a problem.
Lam et al. 2023, Science 382:1416 (GraphCast supplement) · Read: Goodfellow §8.2.4 (cliffs, p. 285) and §10.11.1 (clipping, pp. 409–410)
~1.5 min. Emphasize the diagnostic use: clipping silently hides pathologies, so log the clip rate. This is the first of many "what to watch in Weights & Biases" habits — the tool becomes mandatory today, in the lab.

Part 2 · Recipe

Regularization, compressed to one pass

  • Early stopping on a validation metric is the one regularizer you always use — patience measured in epochs, restore the best checkpoint, never the last.
  • Augmentation encodes invariances — but physical fields have preferred directions: never flip a wind field north–south; the Coriolis force is not flip-invariant.
  • Dropout / weight decay trade variance for bias; at pretraining scale, decay does most of the work and dropout often goes to 0.
  • Double descent, in one line: past the interpolation threshold, bigger models can generalize better — part of why foundation models are big at all.
Belkin et al. 2019, PNAS 116:15849 (double descent) · Read: Goodfellow §7.8, pp. 241–249 — it proves early stopping ≈ an ℓ₂ penalty whose strength is set by the stopping time
~2 min. The augmentation bullet is the geoscience twist — invariances are physical claims, not free lunch. Early stopping foreshadows Part 3: "early stopping is only as honest as the validation set it watches — hold that thought."

Part 3 · 25 min

Leakage-safe evaluation

The geoscience failure mode: your test set is not new data — it's your training set, shifted 5 km or 1 day.

Timing: minutes 25–50 — the headline block; protect it. Claim to establish: in environmental data, random splits do not measure generalization, and the error is not small or subtle — it flips paper conclusions. Everything this course grades — research notes, labs, projects — will be held to the standard set in the next ten slides.

Part 3 · 25 min

Environmental samples are not independent — nearby pixels share their cause

  • Tobler’s law, quantified: soil moisture, biomass, and temperature have spatial correlation ranges of 10–1000 km, set by rainfall, terrain, and climate zones.
  • Every machine-learning split assumes test points are independent, identically distributed draws. A test pixel 5 km from a training pixel is, statistically, the same draw.
  • The variogram range — the distance where correlation dies — is the scale your split must respect (measured two slides ahead).
  • The model is not cheating; the evaluation is: memorized geography scores as skill.
Tobler 1970 · Roberts et al. 2017, Ecography 40:913
~2.5 min. Anchor with a concrete pair: two soil-moisture stations 8 km apart in Iowa see the same rain, same soil, same season — knowing one IS knowing the other. Then the independence sentence: every off-the-shelf train_test_split call encodes an independence assumption the atmosphere refuses to satisfy.

Part 3 · 25 min

What a random split does to a map

~3 min on this figure alone — it is the core picture of the week. Left: every accent (test) dot has a training neighbor within the correlation range; the dashed 5-km pair is the leak. Right: the shaded block is the fold — every test point is at least a block-width from any training point. Same data, same model, two different questions being asked.

Part 3 · 25 min

Time leaks the same way: today is mostly yesterday

  • Daily surface soil moisture: correlation between one day and the next ≈ 0.9–0.95; drydowns persist for days to weeks. Streamflow and land-surface temperature behave the same way.
  • Random split over days: a test day sandwiched between two training days is scored on interpolating memory, not modeling physics.
  • Seasonality is a second leak channel — the same phase of the annual cycle recurs in training even when the exact day is held out.
  • Consequence: temporal splits must be contiguous blocks (whole years), with a gap at the seam.
~2 min. Concrete test: "predict soil moisture for July 15 given July 14 and July 16 in training" — persistence alone scores brilliantly, no model needed. Whenever a paper reports random-in-time cross-validation on daily data, its skill ceiling is set by autocorrelation, not by the model.

Part 3 · 25 min

Random splits lie — and the lie is large

  • Ploton et al. 2020, tropical forest biomass from satellite features: explained variance ≈ 0.53 under random cross-validation → ≈ 0.14 under spatial cross-validation. Same model, same data.
  • The pattern replicates across geospatial machine learning: species distribution, air quality, hydrology (basin models collapse on ungauged basins).
  • Optimism gap ≔ score(random split) − score(blocked split). Report it; don’t hide it.
  • A high random-split score is a claim about interpolation near your stations — nothing more. Today’s lab measures this gap on CAMELS-US.
Ploton et al. 2020, Nat. Commun. 11:4540 · Meyer & Pebesma 2022, Nat. Commun. 13:2208
~2.5 min. The Ploton numbers are the slide — 0.53 to 0.14 is not a correction, it's a different conclusion; the mapped product looked publishable and was largely geography memorization. Define the optimism gap on the board and tell them it is a required row in today's lab table and in every project report this semester.

Part 3 · Measure the leak

The variogram measures how far the leak reaches

γ(d) = ½·E[ (Z(s) − Z(s+d))² ] — half the mean squared difference between points a distance d apart

  • Close points agree → γ small; distant points are unrelated → γ flattens at the sill (the field’s total variance).
  • Range = the distance where the curve reaches the sill — beyond it, points are effectively independent.
  • The range is the number your split needs: blocks smaller than the range still leak.
  • Estimate it from the target itself, or better, from your model’s residuals.
Read: Pebesma & Bivand, Spatial Data Science, ch. 12 (variograms) — free online · estimate with scikit-gstat or gstat; today’s lab computes one for CAMELS residuals.
~2.5 min. One formula, one picture. Read γ aloud: "average squared disagreement between points d apart, halved." Nugget = measurement noise + sub-grid variability; sill = variance of the whole field; range = where knowing one point stops telling you about another. That range becomes the block size on the next design slide.

Part 3 · Derivation · 1 of 2

How much does correlation cost? Variance of a correlated mean

n observations, each Var = σ², neighbors correlated: Corr(xi, xi+k) = ρᵏ (first-order autoregressive form)
Var(x̄) = (1/n²)·ΣiΣj Cov(xi,xj) = (σ²/n²)·[ n + 2·Σk=1n−1 (n−k)·ρᵏ ]
— n diagonal terms, and for each lag k there are (n−k) pairs, each counted twice

  • If ρ = 0 this collapses to the textbook σ²/n. Every ρ > 0 term adds variance the independent formula doesn’t see.
  • The double sum is just bookkeeping: count how many pairs sit at each lag, weight by their covariance σ²ρᵏ.
~2 min. Write the double sum as a matrix picture if the room hesitates: an n×n covariance matrix, σ² on the diagonal, σ²ρ on the first off-diagonals, σ²ρ² on the next, and Var(x̄) is the average of ALL its entries. Counting the off-diagonals by lag gives the (n−k) factor. Next slide: close the sum.

Part 3 · Derivation · 2 of 2

Close the sum: the effective sample size

for n ≫ correlation length, (1 − k/n) ≈ 1: Σk=1n−1(1−k/n)·ρᵏ ≈ Σk≥1 ρᵏ = ρ/(1−ρ) (geometric series again)
⟹ Var(x̄) ≈ (σ²/n)·[1 + 2ρ/(1−ρ)] = (σ²/n)·(1+ρ)/(1−ρ)
define neff by Var(x̄) ≕ σ²/neff: neff = n·(1−ρ)/(1+ρ)  ρ = 0.9 → n/19 · ρ = 0.95 → n/39

  • Ten years of daily observations at one station: n = 3650, ρ ≈ 0.95 → ≈ 94 independent samples. A decade of data carries about three months of information.
  • Error bars computed with n are ~√39 ≈ 6× too tight — “significant” model improvements evaporate under blocked resampling.
  • Blocked cross-validation is the honest approximation: folds separated at the correlation scale behave like independent draws of size ≈ neff.
Read: Wilks, Statistical Methods in the Atmospheric Sciences, ch. 5 §5.2.4 · Hartmann, Objective Analysis notes §6.1.5 (same formula, eq. 6.13b) — linked on the Week 2 page
~2.5 min. Same geometric series as the Adam bias correction — point that out, it earns a laugh and cements both. Do the 3650/39 ≈ 94 arithmetic live. Then connect: leakage inflates the score, autocorrelation inflates the confidence — random splits get BOTH wrong at once.

Part 3 · Design

Spatial-block design: block at or beyond the correlation range

  • Estimate the range from a variogram of the target (or model residuals); block size ≥ range.
  • Assign whole blocks to folds; add a buffer strip around test blocks if the range is uncertain.
  • Match block geometry to the deployment question: random blocks → “new nearby areas”; leave-region-out → “new climates” (extrapolation).
  • Too-small blocks re-admit leakage; too-large blocks starve training. There is no free choice — report the block size with the result.
Roberts et al. 2017, Ecography 40:913 · Meyer et al. 2019 (spatial variable selection) · hands-on tutorials: the CAST package site (hannameyer.github.io/CAST)
~2 min. The third bullet is the intellectual center: the split IS the scientific question. "How well does this model do?" is ill-posed — do you mean 10 km away or on a new continent? Choose the split that matches the claim you intend to publish.

Part 3 · Design

Temporal holdout: whole years, and mind the seams

  • Hold out contiguous years — never shuffled days, never shuffled months.
  • Leave a gap at the seam ≥ the system’s memory: days for weather, weeks for soil moisture, months for groundwater.
  • Nonstationarity is a feature of the test: held-out years should contain unseen extremes (e.g., a drought year the model never trained on).
  • Normalization statistics from training years only — computing the climatology over all years is the classic silent leak.
~2 min. The fourth bullet catches real papers: standardizing with the full-period mean quietly hands the model the test years' climate. In today's lab and every lab after, normalization statistics are computed inside the training fold — the agent will scaffold it; you must verify it.

Part 3 · The standard

WeatherBench2: year separation as the professional standard

  • Common ERA5 ground truth, fixed metrics (root-mean-square error and anomaly correlation on standard fields), strict train/validation/test year separation — every model on the leaderboard obeys it.
  • GraphCast, Pangu-Weather, NeuralGCM, and the European operational forecast are all scored on the same held-out year: that is why the comparison is believable.
Rasp et al. 2024, arXiv:2308.15560 · weatherbench2.readthedocs.io

Part 4 · 10 min

The setup — and three ways the model can cheat

  • Task: daily surface soil moisture from precipitation, land-surface temperature, vegetation index, soil texture, terrain. Labels: ~2,800 stations of the International Soil Moisture Network, heavily clustered in the United States and Europe.
  • Leak 1 · space: many station pairs sit <10 km apart — well inside the correlation range.
  • Leak 2 · time: day-to-day autocorrelation ≈ 0.95 — any shuffled-day split is persistence in disguise.
  • Leak 3 · instrument: each network (SCAN, USCRN, SNOTEL, …) has its own sensors and calibration — network-specific bias is learnable and looks like skill.
  • A random split leaves all three open. A competent reviewer rejects in one paragraph.
International Soil Moisture Network: Dorigo et al. 2021, HESS 25:5749 · validation good practice: Gruber et al. 2020, Remote Sens. Environ. 244:111806
~2.5 min. Leak 3 is the one students miss: hold out stations but not networks, and the model still recognizes "this reads like a SCAN sensor" and exploits the network's calibration offset. Instrument identity is a feature you did not mean to provide.

Part 4 · 10 min

Hold out along every axis the model could exploit

By region

  • Leave whole continents / climate zones out.
  • Tests spatial transfer: “does it work where we have no stations?”
  • Kills Leak 1.

By year

  • Train ≤ 2018, test 2020–21, gap at the seam.
  • Tests temporal transfer under unseen climate anomalies.
  • Kills Leak 2.

By network

  • Fit on SCAN + others, test on USCRN.
  • Tests instrument transfer; bounds calibration-bias learning.
  • Kills Leak 3.
~3 min. One axis per leak — the symmetry is the takeaway. Ask which single axis they'd keep if forced to choose one, and why the answer depends on the deployment claim (a global map product needs region-holdout most; an operational forecast needs year-holdout most).

Part 4 · 10 min

The reviewer-proof protocol is the intersection, not the union

  • Primary score: held-out regions × held-out years simultaneously — unseen place in an unseen year.
  • Secondary: network-holdout to bound the instrument-bias contribution.
  • All tuning (sweeps, early stopping, checkpoint choice) inside training folds; the test partition is touched once.
  • Report a per-region table, not one global mean — the model’s failures in data-sparse regions are the scientific result.
  • This is discussion question 2 in tonight’s entrance ticket. Use today’s vocabulary: range, block, gap, optimism gap.
~3 min, end at minute 60. Stress "touched once": the test set is spent the moment it influences any decision.
